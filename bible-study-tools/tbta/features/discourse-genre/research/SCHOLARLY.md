# Research: Discourse Genre

## Executive Summary

Discourse genre identifies the type of discourse or literary form being used in Biblical texts. The scholarly consensus, particularly following Robert Longacre's influential typology, categorizes discourse into four main functional types: **Narrative** (story with agent focus and temporal succession), **Procedural** (instructions with temporal succession but reduced agent focus), **Behavioral/Hortatory** (commands/exhortations with agent focus but logical rather than temporal succession), and **Expository** (explanatory discourse with neither agent focus nor temporal succession). Biblical texts exhibit additional genre layers including **poetry** (with distinctive parallelism and reduced verbal forms), **prophecy** (combining narrative future events, behavioral commands, and evaluation), **apocalyptic** (highly symbolic visionary literature), **wisdom** (didactic literature with proverbs, reflections, and lyrics), **epistle** (occasional letters with rhetorical structure), and **legal** (combining casuistic case law and apodictic absolute commands).

Discourse genre is critically important for Bible translation because:

1. **Verbal morphology varies by genre**: Hebrew wayyiqtol drives narrative main events, while weqatal appears in predictive/procedural discourse; Greek aorist strings narrative events while aspect functions differ in other genres.

2. **Structural features differ**: Narrative uses plot for coherence; hortatory uses logical arguments; poetry uses parallelism; legal texts combine conditional and absolute forms.

3. **Translation errors occur when genre is misidentified**: Treating Genesis 1 as poetry vs. narrative affects cosmological interpretation; treating apocalyptic symbolism literally produces hermeneutical errors; translating legal casuistic as apodictic changes the force of commands.

4. **Target language genre conventions differ**: Some languages mark narrative/non-narrative grammatically; poetry's parallelism translates well but other features don't; epistolary rhetorical conventions vary cross-linguistically.

Key scholars include **Robert Longacre** (functional discourse typology), **Stephen Levinsohn** (discourse features of Biblical Greek/Hebrew for translation), **Steven Runge** (discourse grammar of Greek NT), **Daniel Wallace** (Greek exegetical syntax), **Bruce Waltke & Michael O'Connor** (Hebrew syntax), **John Beekman & John Callow** (semantic structure in translation), and **Joseph Grimes** (reference tracking and discourse theory).

WALS does not contain specific discourse genre features—it focuses on phonological, grammatical, and lexical properties. Grambank similarly emphasizes morphosyntactic structures rather than discourse-level phenomena, though both databases are used in studies analyzing text types cross-linguistically.

## 1. Scholarly Sources

### Longacre, Robert E. (1996). *The Grammar of Discourse* (2nd ed.)

**Source**: [Springer](https://www.springer.com/gp/book/9780306452352) | [Longacre Genre Analysis Dataset - Logos](https://support.logos.com/hc/en-us/articles/360057224111-Longacre-Genre-Analysis-Dataset)

**Key Findings**:
- Proposes four basic discourse types based on two parameters: Agent Orientation (±AO) and Contingent Temporal Succession (±CTS)
- **Narrative**: +AO, +CTS (story with agents and time progression)
- **Procedural**: -AO, +CTS (how-to instructions focusing on process, not agent)
- **Behavioral/Hortatory**: +AO, -CTS (directives about how people should behave, with logical rather than temporal succession)
- **Expository**: -AO, -CTS (explanatory texts with thematic orientation and logical succession)
- The Longacre Genre Analysis Dataset applies this taxonomy to Old and New Testament pericopes, with primary and secondary genre assignments

**Citation Code**: {longacre-1996-grammar}

**Relevance**: Provides the foundational functional typology used in discourse analysis for Bible translation. Longacre's approach focuses on the **function** of text rather than merely form, cutting across traditional categories like narrative, poetry, and prophecy. For example, biblical prophecies can function as narrative (predictions), behavioral (commands/rebukes), or hortatory (exhortations).

### Longacre, Robert E. (1994). "Weqatal Forms in Biblical Hebrew Prose"

**Source**: In Bergen, Robert D. (ed.), *Biblical Hebrew and Discourse Linguistics*. Winona Lake: Eisenbrauns, pp. 50-98. | [בלשנות](https://balshanut.wordpress.com/2008/02/15/longacre-robert-weqatal-forms-in-biblical-hebrew-prose-in-biblical-hebrew-and-discourse-linguistics-ed-robert-d-bergen-winona-lake-eisenbrauns-1994-pp-50-98/)

**Key Findings**:
- Weqatal predominantly operates in background structures in narrative discourse
- In predictive, procedural, and instructional discourse (non-narrative genres), clause-initial weqatal parallels the clause-initial wayyiqtol in narrative—both describe main events of the storyline
- When isolated weqatal appears mid-narrative, it may mark a pivotal or climactic event
- Demonstrates that verbal forms function differently across discourse genres

**Citation Code**: {longacre-1994-weqatal}

**Relevance**: Critical for understanding how Hebrew verbal morphology is genre-dependent. Translators must recognize that the same verbal form (weqatal) signals different discourse functions in narrative vs. procedural/predictive genres. Misidentifying genre leads to incorrect understanding of verbal force.

### Patton, Matthew H., Putnam, Frederic Clarke, & Van Pelt, Miles V. (2019). *Basics of Hebrew Discourse: A Guide to Working with Hebrew Prose and Poetry*

**Source**: [Zondervan/Logos](https://www.logos.com/product/185055/basics-of-hebrew-discourse-a-guide-to-working-with-hebrew-prose-and-poetry) | [Christianbook](https://www.christianbook.com/basics-hebrew-discourse-working-narrative-poetry/matthew-patton/9780310535768/pd/535768) | [Review - Hebrew Café](https://www.thehebrewcafe.com/main/2020/07/discourse-review/) | [Review - spoiledmilks](https://spoiledmilks.com/2022/03/21/review-basics-of-hebrew-discourse-patton-putnam/)

**Key Findings**:
- Distinguishes discourse analysis (text linguistics, macro-syntax) from traditional grammar (micro-syntax)

- Addresses glosses, meaning, and translation implications

**Citation Code**: {patton-putnam-2019-hebrew-discourse}

**Relevance**: Modern introduction to Hebrew discourse analysis integrating traditional grammatical knowledge with discourse-level interpretation. ### Porter, Stanley E. (1989). *Verbal Aspect in the Greek of the New Testament, with Reference to Tense and Mood*

**Source**: [Koine-Greek Resources](https://koine-greek.com/studies-in-linguistics/verbal-aspect/) | [Article on Aspect](https://andynaselli.com/wp-content/uploads/2007_verbal_aspect.pdf) | [Discussion](https://koine-greek.com/2012/05/31/4058/)

**Key Findings**:
- Argues Greek verbal system indicates verbal aspect (how action unfolds) rather than levels of time (past/present/future)
- Verbal aspect is semantic category by which speaker/writer grammaticalizes perspective on action through tense-form selection
- Time values established at level of larger units (sentence, paragraph, proposition, discourse)
- Temporal reference not intrinsic to tense-forms, including indicative
- Biblical Greek is aspect-prominent language
- In narratives, perfective aspect (aorist) strings sequences of events together

**Citation Code**: {porter-1989-verbal-aspect}

**Relevance**: Critical for understanding Greek verbal system in discourse. Porter's aspectual approach (though debated) demonstrates that verbal forms function differently in narrative discourse than in other genres. Narrative discourse relies heavily on aorist for mainline events, while epistolary and expository discourse use verbal forms differently.

## 2. Typological Databases

### WALS (World Atlas of Language Structures)

**Source**: [WALS Online](https://wals.info/) | [Max Planck Institute](https://www.eva.mpg.de/linguistics/past-research-resources/typological-surveys/the-world-atlas-of-language-structures-wals/) | [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Atlas_of_Language_Structures)

**Database Overview**:
- 142 features (maps with accompanying texts) on structural properties of languages
- Covers phonological, grammatical, and lexical properties
- 2,650 languages shown on maps
- More than 58,000 data points
- Features have 2-28 different values
- 55 authors contributed to database
- Available online at wals.info

**Discourse-Related Features**:
WALS does **not** contain specific features for discourse genre or text types. The database focuses on:
- Phonological features (vowel inventory size, consonant systems)
- Morphological features (case, number, gender marking)
- Syntactic features (word order, passive constructions, noun-genitive order)
- Lexical features (semantic patterns like 'hand'/'arm' polysemy)

**Implication for Discourse Genre**:
While WALS lacks discourse-level features, its morphosyntactic features are relevant for understanding how languages **grammatically encode** information that affects genre translation:

- **Word Order Features** (e.g., Chapter 81-97): Different orders may be preferred in narrative vs. non-narrative discourse
- **Clause Conjunction Features**: How languages mark clause relationships affects discourse coherence
- **Tense-Aspect-Mood Features** (e.g., Chapter 65-79): Critical for understanding how languages encode narrative progression vs. procedural instructions vs. hortatory commands
- **Evidentiality Features** (e.g., Chapter 77): Some languages grammatically mark information source, relevant for prophetic vs. narrative discourse

**Citation Code**: {wals-online}

**Relevance**: While WALS doesn't directly address discourse genre, it provides cross-linguistic data on grammatical features that function differently across genres. Translators can consult WALS to understand how target languages grammatically encode features critical to specific discourse types.

### Grambank

**Source**: Referenced in [Linguistic Typology Journal](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/journal/key/lity/html?lang=en) | [Text Types](https://grokipedia.com/page/Text_types)

**Database Overview**:
- Described as "the largest existing database of morphosyntactic" features
- Used in linguistic typology research
- Focus on morphosyntactic structures

**Discourse-Related Features**:
Like WALS, Grambank focuses primarily on morphosyntactic features rather than discourse-level phenomena. However, research using Grambank has examined:

- Cross-linguistic position of speech verbs relative to direct speech reports in narrative oral texts
- Language documentation corpora from areally balanced sample of 12 languages
- Analysis of narrative text types

**Implication for Discourse Genre**:
Grambank data on morphosyntactic features can be applied to understanding how different languages handle:
- Quotation marking in narrative
- Participant reference in narrative texts
- Verbal morphology relevant to narrative progression

**Citation Code**: {grambank-database}

**Relevance**: Like WALS, Grambank provides morphosyntactic data applicable to discourse analysis but doesn't directly classify discourse genres. Useful for understanding structural differences between languages that affect genre-based translation.

---

### General Note on Typological Databases and Discourse Genre

Neither WALS nor Grambank contains features specifically for discourse genre classification (narrative, procedural, hortatory, expository, poetry, apocalyptic, etc.). This is because:

1. **Discourse genre is functional/pragmatic** rather than purely structural
2. **Genre operates at text level**, while typological databases focus on sentence-level and word-level features
3. **Genre categorization is partially culture-specific**, making cross-linguistic comparison challenging

However, typological databases are **indirectly relevant** because:

- They document grammatical features (TAM, word order, clause conjunction, evidentiality) that function differently across discourse genres
- They provide data on how languages mark narrative progression, backgrounding/foregrounding, participant reference—all genre-relevant phenomena
- They enable translators to predict which discourse features will be obligatory vs. optional in target languages

**Recommendation**: For discourse genre typology, consult:
- **Longacre's functional typology** (narrative, procedural, behavioral, expository)
- **SIL discourse analysis materials** (genre-specific training)
- **Levinsohn's discourse feature analyses** (Hebrew and Greek)
- **Language-specific discourse grammars** rather than broad typological databases

## 3. Translation Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Genesis 1:1-2:3 — Narrative vs. Poetry Debate

**Languages**: Hebrew (source) → English, various translations

**Source**: [Frame-Poythress - Genre of Genesis](https://frame-poythress.org/dealing-with-the-genre-of-genesis-and-its-opening-chapters/) | [Is Genesis Poetry?](https://creation.com/is-genesis-poetry-figurative-a-theological-argument-polemic-and-thus-not-history) | [The Briefing](https://thebriefing.com.au/2011/05/genesis-11-23-is-not-poetry-and-it-is-historical/) | [Boyd Statistical Study](https://digitalcommons.cedarville.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1191&context=icc_proceedings)

**Feature Handling**: The genre of Genesis 1 is debated: is it narrative prose, poetry, or "elevated prose"?

**Evidence for Narrative**:
- **Verbal morphology**: Steven Boyd's statistical study shows distribution of wayyiqtol indicates narrative, not poetry
- **Lack of parallelism**: Main characteristic of Hebrew poetry (parallelism) is absent
- **Lack of figurative language**: Contains little/no tropes, symbolism, or metaphors typical of poetry

**Evidence for Poetry**:
- **Rhythmic structure**: Has rhythmic, symbolically-charged quality unlike simple prose
- **Refrain patterns**: Repetition of "And God said... and it was so... and God saw that it was good"
- **Extrabiblical parallels**: Ancient Near Eastern creation stories typically poetic

**Translation Implications**:

1. **If treated as narrative**: Translators render as straightforward historical account with narrative verbal forms; English translations use past tense narrative: "In the beginning God created..." (ESV, NIV, KJV)

2. **If treated as poetry**: Translators might use poetic formatting, figurative interpretation, present tense in some contexts; some translations format with poetic indentation or allow for non-literal interpretation of days/order

3. **If treated as "elevated prose"**: Translators recognize stylistic distinctiveness while maintaining narrative force; preserve repetitive structure and formulaic elements while using prose formatting

**Specific Verse**: Genesis 1:26 "Let **us** make man in **our** image"

**Genre Impact on Translation**:
- **Narrative interpretation**: The plural pronouns require explanation (Trinity, divine council, etc.); numerical features of pronouns are theologically significant
- **Poetic interpretation**: Might allow more figurative reading, potentially downplaying theological significance of plural forms

**Theological Stakes**: **HIGH**. Genre identification affects:
- Cosmology (age of earth, creation order)
- Christology (Trinitarian theology from "us/our")
- Hermeneutics (literal vs. figurative interpretation elsewhere)

**Citation**: {case-study-genesis1-genre}

### Case Study 2: Acts 16:16-40 — Narrative Discourse Features

**Languages**: Koine Greek (source) → multiple target languages

**Source**: [Nicolle - Narrative Discourse Analysis and Bible Translation: Acts 16:16-40](https://www.academia.edu/33874789/Narrative_Discourse_Analysis_and_Bible_Translation_Training_materials_based_on_Acts_16_16_40) | [SIL Global](https://www.sil.org/resources/publications/entry/69386)

**Feature Handling**: Training materials demonstrate how to identify and translate narrative discourse features

**Key Narrative Features in Acts 16:16-40**:

1. **Episode opening** (v. 16): "Once when we were going to the place of prayer..."
   - Temporal setting + participant introduction
   - Target languages need natural episode-opening conventions

2. **Mainline events**: String of aorist verbs narrating sequential actions
   - Paul exorcises spirit (v. 18)
   - Owners drag Paul/Silas to authorities (v. 19)
   - Magistrates order beating (v. 22)
   - Jailer imprisons them (v. 23)
   - Earthquake occurs (v. 26)
   - Target languages must distinguish mainline from background events

3. **Background information**: Imperfect and pluperfect forms
   - "A slave girl who had a spirit of divination" (v. 16) - character introduction
   - "She followed Paul... crying out" (v. 17) - habitual action
   - Target languages encode backgrounding differently (subordination, verbal forms, word order)

4. **Peak marking** (vv. 25-34): Earthquake, conversion, baptism
   - Heightened detail, direct speech
   - Target languages may use special particles, word order, or repetition to mark peak

5. **Dialogue representation**: Multiple direct speech quotations
   - Slave girl's shout (v. 17)
   - Owners' accusation (vv. 20-21)
   - Paul's command to spirit (v. 18)
   - Jailer's question (v. 30)
   - Target languages vary in quotation marking conventions

**Translation Challenge**: Target language must:
- Use appropriate narrative verbal forms (not procedural or hortatory forms)
- Mark episode boundaries naturally
- Distinguish mainline from background events
- Mark peak appropriately
- Handle direct speech with target language quotation conventions

**Specific Example**: Verse 30 "Sirs, what must I do to be saved?"
- **Genre sensitivity**: This question occurs at narrative peak
- **Translation**: Must preserve urgency and dramatic force
- **Some languages**: Use special question particles or verbal forms at narrative peaks
- **Some languages**: Front the verb for emphasis
- **Error**: Translating with routine question form flattens narrative drama

**Citation**: {case-study-acts16-narrative}

### Case Study 3: Papua New Guinea Minority Languages — Discourse Genre Challenges

**Languages**: Ipili, Lamogai, Ap Ma, Yali (Papua New Guinea) ← Biblical Hebrew/Greek

**Source**: [Minority Language Translation](https://translation.bible/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/clark-1997-minority-language-status-and-attitudes-towards-bible-translation.pdf) | [Bible Translation as Missions](https://www.nobts.edu/baptist-center-theology/journals/journals/JBTM_12-1_Spring_2015.pdf) | [PNG Resources](https://www.wycliffe.net/organisation/papua-new-guinea-bible-translation-association/)

**Case 3a: Ipili Language (Terrance Borchard)**

**Feature Handling**: "Discourse Level Functional Equivalence Translation" (doctoral dissertation, Fuller Theological Seminary)

**Challenge**: Ipili language requires genre-appropriate discourse structures differing from Greek/Hebrew

**Translation Implications**:
- Ipili narrative discourse uses different participant reference tracking than Greek
- Procedural discourse (OT legal texts) requires genre-specific verbal forms in Ipili
- Hortatory discourse (NT epistles) uses distinct clause-linking patterns

**Specific Challenge**: OT law codes mix procedural ("When you build a house...") and apodictic ("You shall not murder") discourse. Ipili must distinguish these with different verbal forms and clause structures.

**Citation**: {case-study-ipili-png}

**Case 3b: Lamogai Language (Dave Brunn)**

**Feature Handling**: 21 years facilitating NT translation into previously unwritten language

**Challenge**: Oral language with no existing literary tradition required developing genre conventions

**Translation Implications**:
- **Narrative genre**: Established natural narrative opening formulas, event sequencing markers, closure formulas
- **Epistle genre**: Developed written conventions for epistolary opening/closing (Lamogai had oral greeting patterns but no written letter conventions)
- **Poetic genre** (Psalms, prophecy): Created written representation of oral poetic features (rhythm, parallelism, imagery)

**Specific Challenge**: Gospel narratives vs. Acts narratives vs. epistolary narrative sections (e.g., Philippians 2:6-11 hymn)
- Required identifying genre shifts and applying appropriate discourse conventions
- Lamogai readers needed to recognize when Paul shifts from direct exhortation to narrative illustration to poetic hymn

**Case 3c: Translation of Divine Name (31 PNG Languages)**

**Feature Handling**: Case study on translating YHWH in OT across 31 Papua New Guinea translation projects

**Challenge**: How genre affects divine name translation
- **Narrative contexts**: Name functions as participant reference
- **Legal contexts** (Ten Commandments): "You shall not take the name of YHWH your God in vain"—name has special status
- **Poetic contexts** (Psalms): Name used in praise formulas and parallelism
- **Prophetic contexts**: "Thus says YHWH"—messenger formula

**Translation Implications**: Different PNG languages handled divine name differently based on:
- Whether target language has special register for deity names
- How participant reference works in narrative
- Whether legal discourse uses special vocabulary
- How poetic parallelism operates

**Specific Example**: Some languages used borrowed form "Yahweh"; others used indigenous deity names; others used descriptive phrases. Choice interacted with genre:
- **Narrative**: Required name usable for participant tracking
- **Legal/prophetic**: Required name with appropriate authority weight
- **Poetic**: Required name that worked in parallelistic structures

**Case 3d: Yali Language (Oral Culture)**

**Feature Handling**: Accommodating written Bible text in oral culture context

**Challenge**: Yali culture lacks tradition of written narrative/expository texts

**Translation Implications**:
- **Narrative**: Oral narrative uses performer features (intonation, gesture, audience participation) that written text cannot convey
- **Epistle/expository**: Concepts of written argument/explanation foreign to oral rhetorical tradition
- **Legal**: Oral law transmission differs from written law codes
- **Poetry**: Oral poetry uses musical/rhythmic features distinct from written poetic conventions

**Specific Solution**: Translators created "oral Bible" resources alongside written text, using Yali oral discourse conventions for each genre:
- Narrative: Followed Yali storytelling patterns (episode structure, dialogue representation, closure formulas)
- Hortatory: Used Yali persuasive speech patterns
- Poetry: Incorporated Yali song/chant conventions

**Citation**: {case-study-yali-orality}

### Summary of Translation Case Studies

These case studies demonstrate:

1. **Genre misidentification causes translation errors**: Genesis 1 poetry vs. narrative debate affects cosmology and Trinitarian theology

2. **Genre features must be explicitly taught**: Nicolle's Acts 16 training shows translators must consciously identify mainline vs. background, peak marking, episode structure

3. **Target languages require genre-appropriate structures**: PNG case studies show discourse genre affects:
   - Verbal morphology
   - Participant reference
   - Clause linking
   - Quotation marking
   - Peak marking
   - Opening/closing formulas

4. **Oral vs. literate cultures require different approaches**: Yali case shows written/oral distinction interacts with genre categories

5. **Genre shifts within texts must be recognized**: Lamogai translators must identify when Paul shifts from narrative to exhortation to poetic hymn

## 4. Key Biblical Passages Where Discourse Genre Is Critical

### Narrative Discourse

**Genesis 1:1-2:3** — Creation Account
- **Genre Issue**: Narrative vs. poetry vs. elevated prose debate
- **Critical Reason**: Affects cosmology, Trinitarian theology (1:26), creation order
- **Translation Impact**: Verbal forms, formatting, interpretation framework

**Genesis 6-9** — Flood Narrative
- **Genre Feature**: Extended narrative with temporal markers
- **Critical Reason**: Must maintain chronological sequence; mixing genres obscures timeline
- **Translation Impact**: Verbal sequence, episode boundaries

**Exodus 1-15** — Exodus Narrative
- **Genre Feature**: Narrative interspersed with dialogue and poetry (ch. 15)
- **Critical Reason**: Genre shift to poetry in ch. 15 celebrates narrative events
- **Translation Impact**: Must distinguish narrative past from poetic celebration

**Ruth 1-4** — Complete narrative book
- **Genre Feature**: Model narrative structure with clear episodes, dialogue, resolution
- **Critical Reason**: Demonstrates OT narrative conventions throughout
- **Translation Impact**: Episode structure, participant reference, dialogue marking

**Luke 1:1-4** — Prologue
- **Genre Feature**: Historiographic prologue
- **Critical Reason**: Luke identifies his work as researched historical narrative
- **Translation Impact**: Affects how entire Gospel is read (biography vs. myth vs. didactic fiction)

**Acts 1-28** — Church history narrative
- **Genre Feature**: Extended narrative with speeches embedded
- **Critical Reason**: Distinguishing narrative frame from speech content
- **Translation Impact**: Quotation marking, mainline vs. embedded discourse

**Jonah 1-4** — Prophetic narrative
- **Genre Feature**: Unusual—narrative about prophet rather than prophetic oracles
- **Critical Reason**: Genre subversion is part of message (satirical?)
- **Translation Impact**: Must preserve narrative irony

### Poetry Discourse

**Psalms 1-150** — Diverse poetic types
- **Genre Feature**: Parallelism, reduced verbal forms, imagery
- **Critical Reason**: Poetic features carry meaning beyond propositional content
- **Translation Impact**: Preserving or compensating for parallelism, meter, imagery

**Job 3:1-42:6** — Poetic dialogue
- **Genre Feature**: Poetry within prose frame (1-2; 42:7-17)
- **Critical Reason**: Genre shift between prose and poetry affects interpretation
- **Translation Impact**: Formatting, verbal forms, figurative language handling

**Song of Solomon 1-8** — Love poetry
- **Genre Feature**: Lyric poetry with dramatic dialogue
- **Critical Reason**: Poetic imagery (sometimes erotic) must be handled appropriately
- **Translation Impact**: Cultural adaptation of metaphors, dialogue attribution

**Lamentations 1-5** — Acrostic lament poetry
- **Genre Feature**: Alphabetic acrostic structure
- **Critical Reason**: Form carries meaning (completeness, order within chaos)
- **Translation Impact**: Cannot reproduce acrostic in translation; must compensate

**Isaiah 40-55** — Prophetic poetry
- **Genre Feature**: Extended poetic oracles
- **Critical Reason**: Prophetic message conveyed through poetic rather than prose discourse
- **Translation Impact**: Parallelism, imagery, verbal forms differ from prose prophecy

### Prophecy Discourse

**Isaiah 1-39** — Mixed prophetic genres
- **Genre Feature**: Judgment oracles, salvation oracles, narrative sections, symbolic actions
- **Critical Reason**: Genre shifts within prophetic book
- **Translation Impact**: Must distinguish oracle from narrative from vision report

**Ezekiel 1-48** — Vision reports and symbolic actions
- **Genre Feature**: Vision reports (chs. 1-3, 8-11, 37, 40-48), symbolic actions, oracles
- **Critical Reason**: Visionary material uses different discourse features than oracles
- **Translation Impact**: How to mark vision boundaries, represent symbolic elements

**Amos 1-9** — Judgment oracles
- **Genre Feature**: Formulaic oracle structure ("For three transgressions... for four...")
- **Critical Reason**: Prophetic formula must be preserved to show rhetorical pattern
- **Translation Impact**: Repetitive structure, oracle boundaries

**Micah 1-7** — Mixed: judgment, indictment, hope oracles
- **Genre Feature**: Discourse analysis identifies three oracle types with distinct structures
- **Critical Reason**: Oracle type affects how message is understood (doom vs. salvation)
- **Translation Impact**: Must preserve genre markers that identify oracle type

### Apocalyptic Discourse

**Daniel 7-12** — Apocalyptic visions
- **Genre Feature**: Visionary symbolism, otherworldly mediator, cosmic events
- **Critical Reason**: Symbolic discourse must NOT be translated literally
- **Translation Impact**: How to represent symbolism without over-interpreting

**Zechariah 1-8** — Apocalyptic visions with interpretations
- **Genre Feature**: Vision reports followed by angelic interpretation
- **Critical Reason**: Genre includes built-in interpretation (vision + explanation)
- **Translation Impact**: Must preserve distinction between vision and interpretation

**Revelation 1-22** — NT apocalypse
- **Genre Feature**: Highly symbolic visionary literature
- **Critical Reason**: Genre has suffered from literalistic misinterpretation throughout history
- **Translation Impact**: How to signal symbolic nature without domesticating strangeness

### Wisdom Literature

**Proverbs 1-9** — Extended wisdom instructions
- **Genre Feature**: Teaching wisdom with exhortations
- **Critical Reason**: Hortatory/instructional rather than proverbial
- **Translation Impact**: Different verbal forms than short proverbs (chs. 10-31)

**Proverbs 10-31** — Proverbial sayings
- **Genre Feature**: Short, pithy wisdom sayings (often bicolon with parallelism)
- **Critical Reason**: Proverbial form carries authority; not absolute promises
- **Translation Impact**: Must preserve brevity, parallelism, proverbial register

**Ecclesiastes 1-12** — Reflective wisdom
- **Genre Feature**: First-person reflection with recurring refrains ("vanity of vanities")
- **Critical Reason**: Skeptical/pessimistic tone unusual in Scripture; genre signals this is exploration not doctrine
- **Translation Impact**: Key term הֶבֶל (hebel) "vapor, vanity, meaningless"—translation affects entire message

### Epistle Discourse

**Romans 1-16** — Theological epistle
- **Genre Feature**: Occasional letter with extended theological argument
- **Critical Reason**: Epistolary structure (greeting, body, closing) frames doctrinal content
- **Translation Impact**: Recognizing rhetorical questions, argument structure, quotations

**1 Corinthians 1-16** — Problem-response epistle
- **Genre Feature**: Paul addresses series of issues reported to him
- **Critical Reason**: Genre explains abrupt topic shifts
- **Translation Impact**: Episode boundaries, topic shift markers

**Philemon 1-25** — Personal letter
- **Genre Feature**: Brief personal letter with diplomatic rhetoric
- **Critical Reason**: Shortest Pauline letter exemplifies epistolary conventions
- **Translation Impact**: Epistolary opening/closing, rhetorical politeness

**James 1-5** — Hortatory epistle
- **Genre Feature**: Wisdom instruction in epistolary form
- **Critical Reason**: Hortatory discourse in letter format; coherence through embedded cohesive ties (not plot)
- **Translation Impact**: Cohesion markers differ from narrative; must preserve logical flow

**Hebrews 1-13** — Homily/sermon in written form
- **Genre Feature**: Sustained theological argument with hortatory sections
- **Critical Reason**: Not typical letter (no standard opening); more like written sermon
- **Translation Impact**: Recognizing alternation between exposition and exhortation

### Legal Discourse

**Exodus 20:1-17** — Ten Commandments (Apodictic Law)
- **Genre Feature**: Absolute commands ("You shall not...")
- **Critical Reason**: Apodictic form signals non-negotiable commands
- **Translation Impact**: Must use target language's strongest prohibitive forms

**Exodus 21:1-23:19** — Covenant Code (Casuistic and Apodictic)
- **Genre Feature**: Mix of case law ("If... then...") and absolute commands
- **Critical Reason**: Genre shift indicates different force (conditional vs. absolute)
- **Translation Impact**: Must distinguish casuistic conditionals from apodictic absolutes

**Leviticus 1-7** — Procedural legal discourse
- **Genre Feature**: Instructions for sacrificial procedures
- **Critical Reason**: Procedural discourse (how-to instructions)
- **Translation Impact**: Requires procedural verbal forms (infinitives, imperatives, sequence markers)

**Deuteronomy 12-26** — Deuteronomic Code
- **Genre Feature**: Legal corpus in sermonic frame
- **Critical Reason**: Laws presented as Moses' sermon (hortatory frame + legal content)
- **Translation Impact**: Must preserve both hortatory force and legal precision

### Mixed/Complex Genre Passages

**Luke 2:1-20** — Narrative with embedded hymn
- **Genre Feature**: Birth narrative with angelic hymn (vv. 13-14)
- **Critical Reason**: Genre shift from narrative to poetic praise
- **Translation Impact**: Must signal genre shift (formatting, verbal forms)

**Matthew 5-7** — Sermon on the Mount

- **Critical Reason**: Extended non-narrative section within Gospel narrative
- **Translation Impact**: Must mark episode boundary (narrative → teaching → narrative)

**Philippians 2:6-11** — Christ hymn embedded in epistle
- **Genre Feature**: Poetic hymn within hortatory epistle
- **Critical Reason**: Genre shift signals special emphasis
