# Research: Topic-NP (Topic-Prominence)

## Executive Summary

Topic-prominence is a fundamental typological parameter that distinguishes how languages structure information in discourse. Approximately 25-30% of the world's languages (including Japanese, Korean, Mandarin Chinese, and many Austronesian languages) are classified as topic-prominent, where the topic-comment structure takes precedence over subject-predicate organization.

### Top 10 Findings

1. **Foundational Typology (Li & Thompson 1976)**: Languages fall into four types: subject-prominent (English), topic-prominent (Mandarin), both (Japanese/Korean), and neither. Topic-prominent languages explicitly mark what the clause is "about" (topic) separate from who performs the action (subject).

2. **Information Packaging (Lambrecht 1994)**: Information structure reflects the speaker's assumptions about the hearer's knowledge state. Four key categories govern this: presupposition/assertion, identifiability/activation, topic, and focus. Topic is defined as "the already established matter of current concern."

3. **Given-New Distinction (Chafe 1994)**: Language reflects the flow of thought, regulated by prosody in spoken discourse. The "one new idea constraint" suggests clauses typically introduce one new piece of information against a background of given information.

4. **Assumed Familiarity (Prince 1981)**: Information status is best understood as "assumed familiarity" rather than simple givenness. Entities are classified as Brand-new, Unused, Inferrable, or Evoked based on speaker assumptions about hearer knowledge.

5. **Givenness Hierarchy (Gundel, Hedberg & Zacharski)**: Cognitive status encodes manner of accessibility (not degree). Six statuses exist: in focus > activated > familiar > uniquely identifiable > referential > type identifiable. Different referring expressions conventionally signal these statuses.

6. **Topic Continuity (Givón 1983)**: Topicality is a scalar property measured by referential distance (gaps between mentions), potential interference (competing referents), and cataphoric persistence (continued mentions). Cross-linguistic study of Biblical Hebrew, Japanese, English, Spanish, and others reveals universal patterns.

7. **Japanese Wa/Ga Distinction**: The particle wa marks topics (continuing, contrastive, or generic), while ga marks subjects and new information. This distinction is obligatory and semantically significant—translators must determine information structure to select correctly.

8. **Korean Eun/Neun vs I/Ga**: Parallel to Japanese, eun/neun marks topics (old information, contrast), while i/ga marks subjects (new information, focus). The distinction is grammatically mandatory and affects naturalness.

9. **Biblical Source Languages**: Both Biblical Hebrew and Greek New Testament exhibit information structure phenomena. Hebrew uses word order (especially fronting) to mark topic and focus. Greek violates natural information flow through constituent reordering for prominence.

10. **Translation Case Studies**: SIL International's discourse analysis framework for Bible translation explicitly addresses topic-comment structures, information structure, and thematization. Levinsohn's work on Greek NT identifies propositional topics and points of departure as critical for accurate translation into topic-prominent languages.

## 1. Scholarly Sources

### Li, Charles N. & Thompson, Sandra A. (1976). "Subject and Topic: A New Typology of Language"

**Publication**: In Charles N. Li (Ed.), *Subject and topic* (pp. 457-489). New York: Academic Press.

**Citation Code**: {li-thompson-1976}

**Key Findings**:
- Introduces four-way typology: (1) Subject-prominent (English, French), (2) Topic-prominent (Mandarin Chinese, Lahu), (3) Both subject and topic-prominent (Japanese, Korean), (4) Neither (Tagalog—uses different system)
- In subject-prominent languages, sentences favor subject-predicate descriptions; in topic-prominent languages, sentences favor topic-comment descriptions
- Type (iii) languages have two equally important distinct constructions; Type (iv) languages merge subject and topic
- Foundational distinction between grammatical subject (syntactic role) and discourse topic (pragmatic function)

**Relevance**: This is the foundational work establishing topic-prominence as a typological parameter. Essential for understanding why some languages require explicit topic annotation while others can leave it implicit.

**URL**: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/244443556_Subject_and_Topic_A_New_Typology_of_Language

### Lambrecht, Knud (1994). *Information Structure and Sentence Form: Topic, Focus, and the Mental Representations of Discourse Referents*

**Publication**: Cambridge: Cambridge University Press. (Cambridge Studies in Linguistics, Series Number 71)

**Citation Code**: {lambrecht-1994}

**Key Findings**:
- Information structure is governed by rules relating speaker assumptions about hearer knowledge to formal sentence structure
- Four independent but interrelated categories: presupposition and assertion, identifiability and activation, topic, and focus
- Focus defined as "the semantic component of a proposition whereby the assertion differs from the presupposition"
- Topic defined as "the already established 'matter of current concern'" about which information is provided
- Three focus patterns: PREDICATE FOCUS (topic-comment, most common), ARGUMENT FOCUS, and SENTENCE FOCUS (entire sentence as focus)

**Relevance**: Provides comprehensive framework for analyzing topic and focus across languages. Critical for understanding how information structure interfaces with syntax.

**URL**: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/information-structure-and-sentence-form/8CFE9493CC5A61B6DBA7D68E7C73729A

### Prince, Ellen F. (1981). "Toward a Taxonomy of Given-New Information"

**Publication**: In Peter Cole (Ed.), *Radical Pragmatics* (pp. 223-255). New York: Academic Press.

**Citation Code**: {prince-1981}

**Key Findings**:
- Proposes "assumed familiarity" as more accurate than "givenness"
- Three major types: New, Inferrable, and Evoked
- Subcategories: Brand-new (with indefinite article), Unused (hearer knows but not in discourse-model), Inferrable (can be inferred from context)
- Distinction between discourse-related and assumed familiarity-related newness/givenness

**Relevance**: Refined taxonomy essential for determining when constituents qualify as topics (typically not brand-new, often evoked or inferrable).

**URL**: https://www.media.mit.edu/gnl/discourse/summaries/prince.html

### Gundel, Jeanette K., Hedberg, Nancy, & Zacharski, Ron (2010). "Reference and Accessibility from a Givenness Hierarchy Perspective"

**Publication**: *International Review of Pragmatics*, 2(2), 148-168.

**Citation Code**: {gundel-hedberg-zacharski-2010}

**Key Findings**:
- Givenness Hierarchy framework distinguishes six cognitive statuses: in focus, activated, familiar, uniquely identifiable, referential, type identifiable
- Cognitive statuses encode **manner** of accessibility, not degree
- Different referring forms (pronouns, demonstratives, definites, indefinites) conventionally encode different cognitive statuses
- Framework explains cross-linguistic variation in referring expression choice

**Relevance**: Provides precise cognitive framework for understanding how topics must be accessible to hearers. Topics are typically "activated" or "familiar" in this hierarchy.

**URL**: https://brill.com/view/journals/irp/2/2/article-p148_2.xml

### Givón, Talmy (Ed.) (1983). *Topic Continuity in Discourse: A Quantitative Cross-Language Study*

## 2. Typological Databases

### WALS (World Atlas of Language Structures)

**URL**: https://wals.info/

**Citation Code**: {wals}

**Relevant Features**:
- **Feature 81A**: Order of Subject, Object and Verb—indirectly relevant, as topic-prominent languages often have flexible word order
- **Feature 93A**: Position of Interrogative Phrases in Content Questions—relates to information structure and focus
- **Feature 117A**: Predicative Possession—includes "Topic" as a value in Austronesian languages

**Key Finding**: WALS does not have dedicated features specifically on "information structure" or "topic marking" as independent structural features. Topic/focus concepts appear as values or aspects of other grammatical features, particularly word order, case marking, and referential properties.

**Implication**: Lack of dedicated WALS features reflects the fact that topic-prominence is not marked by single discrete morphological feature but rather by constellation of syntactic, morphological, and prosodic features. This makes it challenging to capture in typological database format.

**Coverage**: 2,679 languages in WALS Online; 144 chapters grouped into 11 thematic sections.

**URL**: https://wals.info/ and https://www.eva.mpg.de/linguistics/past-research-resources/typological-surveys/the-world-atlas-of-language-structures-wals/

### Grambank

**URL**: https://grambank.clld.org/

**Citation Code**: {grambank}

**Relevant Features**:
- Database covers 2,467 language varieties
- 195 logically independent features (mostly binary) spanning all morphosyntax subdomains
- 17 grammatical domains: negation, adnominal modification, **participant marking**, tense, aspect, etc.
- Features associated with clausal, nominal, pronominal, and verbal domains

**Key Finding**: While Grambank includes "participant marking" as a grammatical domain, specific features on topic marking were not identified in search results. The database may encode topic-related phenomena under other categories (word order, case marking, etc.).

**Implication**: Like WALS, Grambank's feature set reveals the challenge of capturing topic-prominence as discrete feature. Most useful for identifying languages with features correlated with topic-prominence (flexible word order, case marking systems, etc.).

**Coverage**: 2,467 language varieties; 195 morphosyntactic features.

**URL**: https://grambank.clld.org/ and https://www.eva.mpg.de/linguistic-and-cultural-evolution/research/grambank/

### Cross-linguistic Topic Marker Study

**Source**: Weidert, Alfons (forthcoming). "The Elusive Topic: Towards a Typology of Topic Markers"

**URL**: https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1388685/FULLTEXT01.pdf

**Citation Code**: {weidert-topic-markers}

**Key Findings**:
- Tentative set of topic marker candidates from 80 languages across all linguistic continents
- Topic markers challenge gram approach: don't easily cluster to obligatory use in prototypical contexts
- Can exhibit very high text frequency despite being astonishingly unstable genealogically

**Implication**: Topic markers exist as identifiable grammatical category across diverse languages, but their behavior doesn't fit neatly into traditional grammatical categories. High functional load but typological instability.

**Coverage**: 80 languages from diverse families and geographic areas.

## 3. Translation Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Japanese Bible Translation—Topic Particle Wa

**Language**: Japanese (jpn, Japonic family)
**Population**: ~125 million speakers
**Feature Handling**: Obligatory topic marking with particles wa (topic) vs ga (subject)

**Translation Challenge**: Genesis 1:1

**Hebrew Source**: בְּרֵאשִׁית בָּרָא אֱלֹהִים אֵת הַשָּׁמַיִם וְאֵת הָאָרֶץ
*Bereshit bara Elohim et hashamayim ve'et ha'aretz*
"In-beginning created God [ACC] the-heavens and-[ACC] the-earth"

**English (Subject-Prominent)**: "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth."

**Japanese Translation**: 初めに神が天と地を創造された
*Hajime-ni Kami-**GA** ten-to chi-wo souzou sareta*
Beginning-at God-**SUBJ** heaven-and earth-**OBJ** create did

**Analysis**: Japanese uses **ga** (subject marker), not **wa** (topic marker), because "God" is new information being introduced into the discourse. This is the presentational sentence establishing God as a participant.

**Specific Verse with Contrastive Topic**: John 8:12

**Greek Source**: Πάλιν οὖν αὐτοῖς ἐλάλησεν ὁ Ἰησοῦς
*Palin oun autois elalēsen ho Iēsous*
"Again therefore to-them spoke the Jesus"

**Japanese Translation**: イエスは再び彼らに語られた
*Iesu-**WA** futatabi karera-ni katarareta*
Jesus-**TOPIC** again them-to spoke

**Analysis**: Japanese uses **wa** (topic marker) because Jesus is the continuing topic from previous discourse (context of John 8:1-11). The wa particle signals topic continuity and frames Jesus as the "matter of concern" for this discourse section.

**Key Insight**: Japanese translators must analyze information structure of source text to determine whether participant is:
1. **Brand-new** → ga (subject marker)
2. **Continuing topic** → wa (topic marker)
3. **Contrastive topic** → wa (with contrastive intonation)

This is not optional stylistic choice but grammatically obligatory distinction affecting meaning.

**Source**: https://nihonkibou.wordpress.com/2012/11/10/souseiki-11-studying-japanese-with-the-bible/

### Case Study 2: Korean Bible Translation—Topic vs Subject Markers

**Language**: Korean (kor, Koreanic family)
**Population**: ~82 million speakers
**Feature Handling**: Parallel to Japanese—eun/neun (topic) vs i/ga (subject)

**Topic Markers**: 은 (eun, after consonant), 는 (neun, after vowel)
**Subject Markers**: 이 (i, after consonant), 가 (ga, after vowel)

**Translation Challenge**: Matthew 5:3 (Beatitudes)

**Greek Source**: Μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοὶ τῷ πνεύματι
*Makarioi hoi ptōchoi tō pneumati*
"Blessed the poor in-the spirit"

**Korean Translation**: 심령이 가난한 사람들은 복이 있다
*Simryeong-i gananhan saramdeul-**EUN** bog-i itda*
Spirit-SUBJ poor people-**TOPIC** blessing-SUBJ exist

**Analysis**: Korean uses **eun** (topic marker) on "poor people" because they are established as the topic/frame for the blessing statement. The predicate adjective construction (X wa Y desu in Japanese, X eun Y ida in Korean) requires topic marking, not subject marking.

**Contrast with New Information**: Luke 2:11

**Greek Source**: ὅτι ἐτέχθη ὑμῖν σήμερον σωτήρ
*hoti etechthē hymin sēmeron sōtēr*
"that was-born to-you today savior"

**Korean Translation**: 오늘 너희를 위하여 구세주가 나셨으니
*Oneul neohuireul wihayeo guseju-**GA** nasyeossuni*
Today you-for savior-**SUBJ** was-born

**Analysis**: Korean uses **ga** (subject marker) because the savior is brand-new information being announced. This is presentational/thetic sentence introducing new participant.

**Key Insight**: Like Japanese, Korean requires translators to distinguish:
- **Old/established information** → eun/neun (topic)
- **New/focused information** → i/ga (subject)
- **Contrastive topic** → eun/neun (with contrast)

**Source**: https://medium.com/story-of-eggbun-education/basic-topic-marker-은-eun-는-neun-vs-subject-marker-이-i-가-ga-48d20f1008d9

### Case Study 3: Mandarin Chinese Bible Translation—Topic-Comment Structure

**Language**: Mandarin Chinese (cmn, Sino-Tibetan family)
**Population**: ~1.2 billion speakers
**Feature Handling**: Prototypical topic-prominent language; topic-comment structure dominant

**Structural Pattern**: TOPIC + COMMENT (where comment is full clause making statement about topic)

**Translation Challenge**: Psalm 118:22

**Hebrew Source**: אֶבֶן מָאֲסוּ הַבּוֹנִים הָיְתָה לְרֹאשׁ פִּנָּה
*Even maasu habonim hayetah lerosh pinah*
"Stone rejected the-builders became to-head corner"

**English (Subject-Prominent)**: "The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone."

**Chinese Translation** (Union Version): 匠人所棄的石頭已成了房角的頭塊石頭
*Jiàngrén suǒ qì de shítou yǐ chéng le fángjiǎo de tóu kuài shítou*

**Structure Analysis**:
- **TOPIC**: 匠人所棄的石頭 *jiàngrén suǒ qì de shítou* "builders REL reject DE stone" = "the stone that builders rejected"
- **COMMENT**: 已成了房角的頭塊石頭 *yǐ chéng le fángjiǎo de tóu kuài shítou* "already become ASP corner DE head CL stone" = "has become the cornerstone"

The topic (stone) is fronted and established, then the comment makes a statement about it. This matches the Hebrew fronted constituent structure.

**Object-Fronting as Topic**: Matthew 6:24

**Greek Source**: οὐ δύνασθε θεῷ δουλεύειν καὶ μαμωνᾷ
*ou dynasthe theō douleuein kai mamōna*
"Not you-are-able God to-serve and mammon"

**Chinese Translation**: 你們不能又事奉神，又事奉瑪門
*Nǐmen bù néng yòu shìfèng Shén, yòu shìfèng Mǎmén*
"You not can both serve God, both serve Mammon"

**Analysis**: Chinese naturally accommodates both objects (God, Mammon) as contrastive topics through the 又...又... *yòu...yòu* "both...and" construction. The parallel structure highlights the contrast between the two potential objects of service.

**Key Insight**: Mandarin Chinese topic-comment structure often provides natural translation for:
1. Hebrew fronted constituents (which often mark topic/focus)
2. Greek left-dislocations and fronted objects
3. Contrastive constructions

Chinese translators must identify which constituents function as discourse topics vs subjects to produce natural, idiomatic Chinese that accurately reflects source text information structure.

**Source**: https://mangolanguages.com/resources/learn/grammar/mandarin-chinese/what-are-topic-comment-structures-in-mandarin-chinese and https://www.researchgate.net/publication/235852523_Topic_and_Topic-Comment_Constructions_in_Mandarin_Chinese

## 4. Key Biblical Passages Where Topic-NP Is Critical

### 4.1. Trinitarian Passages (Non-Arbitrary Theological)

**Genesis 1:26** — "Let **us** make man in **our** image"

**Topic-NP Concern**: In topic-prominent languages with trial number, topic marking affects how the plural/trial reference to God is understood.

**Information Structure**:
- Hebrew: נַעֲשֶׂה אָדָם *na'aseh adam* "let-us-make man" (cohortative plural)
- The plural reference is the critical theological element
- Topic-prominent languages must determine: Is "us" the topic being reintroduced/continued, or new contrastive information?

**Translation Impact**:
- **Japanese**: 我々が *wareware-ga* (we-SUBJ) vs 我々は *wareware-wa* (we-TOPIC)
  - GA would mark this as new/focused information (appropriate for introducing divine plurality)
  - WA would mark continuing topic (inappropriate if first mention of divine plurality)

**Theological Stakes**: HIGH—relates to Trinity doctrine. Misidentifying information status could obscure the significance of the plural reference.

---

**John 1:1** — "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and **the Word was God**"

**Topic-NP Concern**: The final clause "the Word was God" has critical topic-comment structure.

**Information Structure**:
- Greek: θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος *theos ēn ho logos*
- Word order: [PREDICATE NOMINATIVE] [VERB] [SUBJECT]
- Anarthrous θεός (without article) = predicate nominative (qualitative)
- Articular ὁ λόγος (with article) = subject
- This word order indicates: TOPIC = the Word (established from previous clause); COMMENT = was God (new predication about the Word)

**Translation Impact**:
- **Mandarin Chinese**: Topic-comment structure naturally accommodates: 道就是神 *Dào jiù shì Shén* "Word TOPIC-EMPH is God"
- **Japanese**: 言は神であった *Kotoba-WA kami-de atta* "Word-TOPIC God was"
- **Korean**: 말씀은 하나님이시니라 *Malsseum-EUN Hananim-isimnida* "Word-TOPIC God is"

All three topic-prominent languages naturally use topic markers on "Word," accurately reflecting the Greek information structure.

**Theological Stakes**: CRITICAL—Jehovah's Witnesses mistranslate as "the Word was **a** god" (polytheism). Proper topic-comment analysis supports orthodox rendering "the Word was God" (qualitative predicate nominative).

---

### 4.2. Beatitudes (Predicate Adjective Topic-Comment)

**Matthew 5:3-11** — "Blessed are the poor in spirit... Blessed are those who mourn..."

**Topic-NP Concern**: Each beatitude is predicate adjective construction with fronted adjective.

**Information Structure**:
- Greek: Μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοί *Makarioi hoi ptōchoi* "Blessed the poor"
- Fronted predicate adjective (focus position in Greek)
- Subject (the poor, those who mourn, etc.) is TOPIC; "blessed" is COMMENT/PREDICATION

**Translation Impact**:
- **Japanese**: 心の貧しい人々は幸いです *Kokoro-no mazushii hitobito-WA shiawai desu* "Heart-GEN poor people-TOPIC blessed are"
- **Korean**: 심령이 가난한 자는 복이 있나니 *Simryeong-i gananhan ja-NEUN bog-i innani* "Spirit-SUBJ poor person-TOPIC blessing-SUBJ exists"

Topic markers on the subject (poor, mourning, etc.) accurately reflect that these are the established topics about which blessing is predicated.

**Theological Stakes**: MEDIUM—Affects emphasis and flow of discourse. Topic-comment structure highlights each group receiving blessing, appropriate for this list structure.

---

### 4.3. Contrastive Topics

**John 3:11** — "**We** speak of what we know... but **you** do not receive our testimony"

**Topic-NP Concern**: Contrastive topic construction (we vs you).

**Information Structure**:
- Greek: ἡμεῖς λαλοῦμεν... καὶ τὴν μαρτυρίαν ἡμῶν οὐ λαμβάνετε
- Fronted pronouns ἡμεῖς (we) and implied ὑμεῖς (you) create contrast
- Both are TOPICS in contrastive relationship

**Translation Impact**:
- **Japanese**: 私たちは語るが、あなたがたは私たちの証しを受け入れない
  *Watashitachi-WA kataru ga, anatagata-WA watashitachi-no akashi-WO ukeirenai*
  "We-TOPIC speak but, you-TOPIC we-GEN testimony-OBJ receive-not"

Both pronouns take WA (topic marker), signaling contrastive topic relationship.

**Theological Stakes**: MEDIUM—Affects rhetorical force of Jesus's rebuke. Contrastive topic structure emphasizes the irony: we (who know) speak, but you (who should listen) reject.

---

### 4.4. Topic Continuity in Narrative

**John 8:12-59** — Extended dialogue with "Jesus" as continuing topic

**Topic-NP Concern**: Participant tracking and topic continuity throughout discourse unit.

**Information Structure**:
- Jesus introduced in 8:12: Πάλιν οὖν αὐτοῖς ἐλάλησεν ὁ Ἰησοῥς *Palin oun autois elalēsen ho Iēsous* "Again therefore to-them spoke the Jesus"
- Subsequent references vary: pronouns, zero anaphora, full NP
- Topic continuity maintained throughout 47 verses

**Translation Impact**:
- **Japanese**: Pro-drop language allows zero anaphora for continuing topics
  - 8:12: イエスは *Iesu-WA* (Jesus-TOPIC) — full NP + topic marker for (re)staging
  - 8:14: ∅は *WA* — zero subject + topic marker for continuation
  - 8:21: また∅は *mata WA* "again [Jesus]-TOPIC" — zero with resumptive marker

- **Korean**: Similar pattern with eun/neun marking continued topic, zero anaphora allowed

**Theological Stakes**: LOW (for topic continuity itself)—But critical for maintaining discourse coherence. Misidentifying topic continuity leads to confusion about who is speaking/acting.

---

### 4.5. Topic Shift Marking

**Luke 15:11-32** — Parable of Prodigal Son (topic shifts between father, younger son, older son)

**Topic-NP Concern**: Managing multiple participants and topic shifts in extended narrative.

**Information Structure**:
- 15:11 — Initial setup: Ἄνθρωπός τις εἶχεν δύο υἱούς "A-man certain had two sons" (PRESENTATIONAL)
- 15:12 — Younger son introduced: ὁ νεώτερος *ho neōteros* "the younger" (NEW TOPIC)
- 15:13 — Continued: ὁ νεώτερος υἱός *ho neōteros huios* "the younger son" (TOPIC CONTINUITY)
- 15:20 — Topic shift to father: ὁ πατὴρ αὐτοῦ *ho patēr autou* "the father his" (TOPIC SHIFT)
- 15:25 — Topic shift to older son: Ἦν δὲ ὁ υἱὸς αὐτοῦ ὁ πρεσβύτερος *Ēn de ho huios autou ho presbyteros* "Was now the son his the older" (TOPIC SHIFT with δέ particle)

**Translation Impact**:
- **Japanese/Korean**: Topic shifts require explicit marking
  - New topic → full NP + wa/neun
  - Continued topic → pronoun/zero + wa/neun
  - Contrastive topic shift → full NP + wa/neun with contrastive intonation

- Particles like δέ in Greek correlate with topic shift markers in topic-prominent languages

**Theological Stakes**: LOW—But essential for narrative coherence. Incorrect topic management confuses which character is in focus at each point.

---

### 4.6. Generic/Gnomic Statements

**Proverbs 3:5-6** — "Trust in the LORD with all your heart..."

**Topic-NP Concern**: Generic second-person address as topic.

**Information Structure**:
- Hebrew: בְּטַח אֶל־יְהוָה בְּכָל־לִבֶּךָ *betach el-YHWH bechol-libekha* "Trust in-LORD with-all your-heart"
- Implied second-person subject (you) is generic topic
- Imperative mood but topic-comment structure: "As for you, trust in the LORD"

**Translation Impact**:
- **Mandarin Chinese**: Topic-comment natural for generic wisdom:
  你要專心仰賴耶和華 *Nǐ yào zhuānxīn yǎnglài Yēhéhuá* "You-TOPIC must wholeheartedly rely-on LORD"

- Topic pronoun "you" establishes addressee as topic for entire proverbial instruction

**Theological Stakes**: LOW—But affects didactic force. Topic-comment structure frames instruction as wisdom applicable to the addressee.

---

### 4.7. Summary: Passages Requiring Topic-NP Analysis

| Passage | Topic-NP Type | Information Structure Challenge | Theological Stakes |
|---------|---------------|----------------------------------|-------------------|
| **Gen 1:26** | Contrastive/New | Plural "us" reference to God | HIGH (Trinity) |
| **John 1:1** | Predicate nominative | "The Word was God" topic-comment | CRITICAL (Deity of Christ) |
| **Matt 5:3-11** | Predicate adjective | Beatitudes topic-comment structure | MEDIUM (Emphasis) |
| **John 3:11** | Contrastive topic | We vs you contrast | MEDIUM (Rhetorical force) |
| **John 8:12-59** | Topic continuity | Extended dialogue with Jesus as topic | LOW (Coherence) |
| **Luke 15:11-32** | Topic shifts | Multiple participants, topic management | LOW (Narrative clarity) |
| **Prov 3:5-6** | Generic topic | Second-person generic wisdom | LOW (Didactic frame) |
| **Ps 118:22** | Fronted object | "Stone" as topic, "rejected" as comment | MEDIUM (Messianic prophecy) |

**Pattern**: Topic-NP analysis most critical for:
1. **Theological content** where participant reference affects doctrine (Trinity, Christology)
2. **Predicate constructions** where topic-comment structure differs from subject-predicate
3. **Contrastive contexts** where participants are contrasted
4. **Discourse structure** where topic continuity/shift affects coherence
