# Honorifics/Register: Research Synthesis

## Feature Definition

**Honorifics/Register** (also: Speaker Demographics, Social Register) encodes grammatically required social relationships, relative status, age differences, formality levels, and politeness marking in speech. The feature addresses how speakers mark social distance, respect, intimacy, and hierarchy through mandatory linguistic forms (pronouns, verb endings, vocabulary choices, particles).

### Language Typology (See: LANGUAGES.md)

**Global Distribution**: ~25% of world languages grammatically require honorific marking (WALS Feature 45A).

**Mandatory Systems** (obligatory, multiple levels):
- **Japanese** (4+ levels: casual/polite/honorific/humble)
- **Korean** (7 historical, 4 modern levels)
- **Hindi** (3 levels: tū/tum/āp + verb agreement)
- **Bengali** (3 levels, cognate to Hindi)
- **Thai** (particles + royal vocabulary)
- **Javanese** (3 levels: ngoko/madya/krama with lexical replacement)

**Optional Systems** (culturally important but grammatically flexible):
- **Spanish, French, German, Russian** (T/V binary distinction)
- **Mandarin Chinese** (你/您 ni/nin)
- **Indonesian, Tagalog** (lexical/particle-based)
- **Vietnamese** (kinship-based pronouns)
- **Tamil, Malayalam** (plural-based honorifics)

**Absence**: English, Swahili core, most African/Pacific languages

**Candidate languages for Stage 2**: Japanese, Mandarin, Hindi, Thai, Bengali, Spanish, Vietnamese, Tamil, Indonesian, Javanese

### Scholarly Research (See: SCHOLARLY.md)

**Foundational Theories**:
- **Brown & Gilman (1960)**: Power/Solidarity framework for T/V distinctions
- **Brown & Levinson (1987)**: Face theory; honorifics as negative politeness strategies
- **Ide (1989)**: Wakimae (discernment) theory: East Asian politeness obligatory, not strategic
- **Comrie (1976)**: Three honorific axes—Speaker-Addressee (most common), Speaker-Referent, Speaker-Bystander

**Typological Patterns**:
- **East Asia** (Japan, Korea, Javanese): Complex multi-level systems with speaker-referent marking
- **Southeast Asia** (Thai, Vietnamese): Particle-based or kinship pronoun systems
- **South Asia** (Hindi, Bengali): Pronoun + verb agreement systems
- **Europe** (Romance, Germanic, Slavic): Binary T/V distinctions
- **Bantu**: Age-specific kinship terms (kaka mkubwa vs. kaka mdogo)

**37+ Scholarly Sources Cited** with URLs; case studies from Japanese, Korean, French translations.

**Key finding**: Biblical source languages (Hebrew, Greek) lack honorific morphology, forcing target language translators to infer social relationships from context.

## Key Discrepancies & Clarifications

**TBTA vs. Research Alignment**:
1. **Gender/Relationship sub-features**: TBTA documentation lists these in count (6 features) but provides NO documented values. Linguistic research confirms gender-marked speech exists (Japanese wa/ne vs. zo/na) but needs language-specific encoding.

2. **Theological stakes**: TBTA marks feature as "Complete" and shows basic examples; our theological analysis identifies HIGH-stakes doctrinal contexts (divine address, Christology, blasphemy) requiring systematic guidance beyond TBTA's basic documentation.

3. **Source language encoding**: TBTA correctly focuses on target language needs (Hebrew/Greek neutral); our research confirms neither language has T/V distinction or grammatical honorifics.

4. **Discernment theory**: TBTA applies feature mechanically; Ide's wakimae theory (1989) explains why East Asian systems are OBLIGATORY (discernment-based), not optional stylistic choices.
