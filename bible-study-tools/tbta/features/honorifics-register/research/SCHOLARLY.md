# Scholarly Research: Honorifics-Register

## Executive Summary

Honorifics and register systems represent sociolinguistic mechanisms that encode relative social status, formality, politeness, and respect through grammatical and lexical choices. These systems vary dramatically across languages, from simple binary T/V distinctions in European languages to highly complex multi-level systems in Japanese, Korean, and Javanese.

**Key Linguistic Dimensions**: Research identifies three primary axes of honorific marking: (1) speaker-addressee relationships (politeness/formality), (2) speaker-referent relationships (respect toward third parties), and (3) register/style variation (situational formality). These dimensions are grammatically obligatory in approximately 25% of the world's languages.

**Biblical Translation Challenges**: The source languages (Hebrew and Greek) lack formal honorific systems, creating translation challenges for target languages that grammatically require honorific marking. Translators must make non-arbitrary decisions about: (1) addressing God (formal vs. intimate), (2) Jesus-disciple relationships (familiar vs. respectful), (3) Jesus-Pharisee confrontations (respect vs. rebuke), and (4) social hierarchies in parables (master-servant, elder-youth dynamics).

**Typological Distribution**: East Asian languages (Japanese, Korean, Javanese) have the most elaborate systems with 3-7 speech levels. Southeast Asian languages (Thai, Vietnamese, Tagalog) use particle-based systems. South Asian languages (Hindi, Urdu, Bengali) employ pronoun-based formality. European languages maintain T/V pronoun distinctions. African Bantu languages require age-marking in kinship terms, indirectly affecting register choices.

**Theological Stakes**: While most register choices are stylistic (arbitrary ~85%), certain contexts have theological significance: addressing God reflects intimacy vs. transcendence theology; plural pronouns in Genesis 1:26 affect Trinitarian doctrine; respectful forms for Jesus impact Christological understanding.

**Cross-Linguistic Patterns**: Brown & Levinson's politeness theory (1987) and Brown & Gilman's power-solidarity framework (1960) provide universal frameworks, while Ide's discernment theory (1989) highlights cultural variation in how politeness is conceptualized and enacted.

## 1. Scholarly Sources

### 1.1 Foundational Politeness Theory

#### Brown, Roger & Gilman, Albert (1960). "The Pronouns of Power and Solidarity"

**Publication**: In Thomas A. Sebeok (ed.), _Style in Language_, pp. 253-276. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.

**Key Findings**:
- Introduced T/V distinction terminology from Latin _tū_ (informal singular) and _vōs_ (formal/plural)
- Identified two semantic dimensions: **power** (asymmetric relationships) and **solidarity** (symmetric relationships based on shared characteristics)
- Historical shift from power-based to solidarity-based pronoun usage in 20th century
- Studied French, Italian, Spanish, and German pronoun systems
- Power relationships are non-reciprocal: superior uses T downward, subordinate uses V upward
- Solidarity relationships are reciprocal: mutual T (intimate) or mutual V (formal)

**Citation Code**: {brown-gilman-1960}

**Relevance**: Provides foundational framework for understanding binary honorific distinctions and their social motivations. Essential for analyzing T/V systems in European languages in Bible translation.

**Source**: https://www.academia.edu/116858397/The_Pronouns_of_Power_and_Solidarity
**WALS Reference**: https://wals.info/refdb/record/Brown-and-Gilman-1960

---

#### Brown, Penelope & Levinson, Stephen C. (1987). _Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage_

**Publication**: Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.

**Key Findings**:
- **Face theory**: Positive face (desire for approval) vs. negative face (desire for autonomy)
- **Politeness strategies**: Four main types:
  1. Bald on-record (direct, no mitigation)
  2. Positive politeness (emphasize solidarity, friendliness)
  3. Negative politeness (emphasize deference, formality)
  4. Off-record (indirect, ambiguous)
- Face-threatening acts (FTAs) require mitigation strategies
- Universal principles of politeness with language-specific realizations
- Honorifics serve primarily as negative politeness strategies (showing deference)

**Citation Code**: {brown-levinson-1987}

**Relevance**: Explains why different Bible translation contexts require different politeness strategies. Prayer to God, Jesus teaching disciples, and confrontations with Pharisees represent different face-management situations requiring distinct honorific choices.

**Source**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politeness_theory
**Academic Review**: https://www.ej-lang.org/index.php/ejlang/article/view/137

---

#### Agha, Asif (2007). _Language and Social Relations_

**Publication**: Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.

**Key Findings**:
- Introduced concept of **enregisterment**: how linguistic repertoires become recognized as socially meaningful registers
- Registers are not inherent in linguistic forms but emerge through social practice and metapragmatic labeling
- Honorific systems exemplify enregisterment: forms acquire indexical links to social identities and relationships
- Register variation involves lexical, morphological, syntactic, and phonological dimensions
- Historical processes shape contemporary register systems

**Citation Code**: {agha-2007}

**Relevance**: Provides theoretical framework for understanding honorifics as socially constructed register systems. Explains why translators must consider not just linguistic forms but their social meanings in target cultures.

**Source**: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/language-and-social-relations/CC9D22E0C2DF13A5016C823F457745B7
**Journal Review**: https://www.jstor.org/stable/43104159

### 1.2 Cross-Linguistic Typology

#### Comrie, Bernard (1976). "Linguistic Politeness Axes: Speaker-Addressee, Speaker-Referent, Speaker-Bystander"

**Publication**: Pragmatics Microfiche 1.7:A3.

**Key Findings**:
- Identified three axes of politeness/honorific marking:
  1. **Speaker-addressee**: formality level toward listener (most common)
  2. **Speaker-referent**: respect toward person mentioned (honorific vs. humble forms)
  3. **Speaker-bystander**: register adjustment for overhearers
- Languages vary in which axes they grammaticalize
- Japanese and Korean mark all three axes; European languages primarily mark speaker-addressee

**Citation Code**: {comrie-1976}

**Relevance**: Essential for understanding multi-dimensional honorific systems. Bible translation must consider not just addressing God/Jesus (speaker-addressee) but also referring to them in third person (speaker-referent).

**Source**: Referenced in https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-variation-and-change/article/abs/contemporary-japanese-attitudes-toward-honorifics-keigo/7D8661D5252590D1F9BD28D1C2DF5F10

### 1.3 Japanese Honorific System

#### Ide, Sachiko (1989). "Formal Forms and Discernment: Two Neglected Aspects of Universals of Linguistic Politeness"

**Publication**: _Multilingua_ 8(2/3): 223-248.

**Key Findings**:
- Introduced **wakimae** (discernment) vs. **volition** distinction
- **Discernment**: obligatory choice based on social relationships (Japanese emphasis)
- **Volition**: strategic choice for face management (Western emphasis)
- Japanese honorifics encode social relationships systematically, not just strategic politeness
- Rank and role distinctions are grammatically obligatory in Japanese
- Challenges Brown & Levinson's universalist approach by showing cultural variation

**Citation Code**: {ide-1989}

**Relevance**: Explains why Japanese Bible translations must systematically encode Jesus-disciple, Jesus-Pharisee, and human-divine relationships. Not strategic choices but grammatical requirements based on discernment.

**Source**: https://www.academia.edu/26835615/Japanese_sociolinguistics_politeness_and_womens_language
**ERIC Document**: https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED395525.pdf

---

#### Ide, Sachiko & Yoshida, Megumi (2005). "Sociolinguistics: Honorifics and Gender Differences"

**Publication**: In _The Handbook of Japanese Linguistics_, edited by Shigeru Miyagawa, pp. 444-480. Oxford: Blackwell.

**Key Findings**:
- Gender differences in honorific usage patterns
- Female speakers traditionally use more polite forms (teineigo)
- Male speakers use more assertive/casual forms
- Modern convergence but distinctions persist in formal contexts
- Interaction between gender and honorific levels

**Citation Code**: {ide-yoshida-2005}

**Relevance**: Impacts translation of speech by male vs. female characters in Bible narratives. Cultural expectations about gendered speech may affect reception of translations.

**Source**: https://www.blackwellpublishing.co.uk/content/BPL_Images/Content_Store/WWW_Content/9780367580711/016.pdf

#### Okamoto, Shigeko & Shibamoto Smith, Janet S. (Eds.) (2004). _Japanese Language, Gender, and Ideology: Cultural Models and Real People_

**Publication**: New York: Oxford University Press.

**Key Findings**:
- Keigo (honorifics) system overview: sonkeigo, kenjōgo, teineigo
- **Sonkeigo** (尊敬語): respectful language elevating the subject
- **Kenjōgo** (謙譲語): humble language lowering the speaker/in-group
- **Teineigo** (丁寧語): polite language using -masu/-desu endings
- Honorifics reflect and construct social relationships
- Ideological aspects of honorific usage

**Citation Code**: {okamoto-smith-2004}

**Relevance**: Three-way honorific system essential for Japanese Bible translation. Jesus must use kenjōgo when speaking to God, teineigo when teaching, and receive sonkeigo from disciples.

**Source**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorific_speech_in_Japanese

### 1.4 Korean Honorific System

#### Sohn, Ho-Min (1999). _The Korean Language_

**Publication**: Cambridge Language Surveys. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.

**Key Findings**:
- **Seven speech levels** in Korean (historically)
- Four commonly used levels in modern Korean:
  1. Hasipsio-che (hasipsio-style, formal polite)
  2. Hao-che (hao-style, formal)
  3. Haeyo-che (haeyo-style, informal polite)
  4. Hae-che (hae-style, informal casual)
- **Jondaemal** (존댓말): formal/respectful speech
- **Banmal** (반말): informal/casual speech
- Honorific verbal suffixes: -si- (subject honorific marker)
- Special honorific vocabulary (e.g., _meogsida_ vs. _meokda_ 'eat')

**Citation Code**: {sohn-1999}

**Relevance**: Korean's grammatically obligatory speech level system requires systematic decisions for every utterance in Bible translation. Cannot remain "neutral" as Hebrew/Greek do.

**Source**: https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Korean_Language.html?id=Sx6gdJIOcoQC

---

#### Sohn, Ho-Min (2001). "Evolution of Korean Honorifics: A Grammaticalization Perspective"

**Publication**: _Korean Linguistics_ 11: 1-26.

**Key Findings**:
- Historical development from Old Korean to Contemporary Korean
- Three interdependent forces driving evolution:
  1. **Structural**: agglutinative, predicate-final typology enables honorific affixes
  2. **Socio-cultural**: Confucian hierarchy values embedded in language
  3. **Interactional**: pragmatic needs in face-to-face communication
- Grammaticalization processes: lexical items → grammatical markers
- Honorific particles emerged through grammaticalization

**Citation Code**: {sohn-2001}

**Relevance**: Explains why Korean honorifics are grammatically entrenched, not optional stylistic choices. Impacts translation philosophy for Korean Bible versions.

**Source**: https://benjamins.com/catalog/kl.17.2.02soh
**Related Research**: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318656513_Honorifics_and_politeness_in_Korean

### 1.5 Thai and Southeast Asian Systems

#### Iwasaki, Shoichi & Ingkaphirom, Preeya (2005). _A Reference Grammar of Thai_

**Publication**: Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.

**Key Findings**:
- Personal pronoun variation encodes respect: _phŏm_ (formal male), _dichǎn_ (formal female), _chǎn_ (informal), _kuu_ (casual)
- **Final particles** essential for politeness:
  - _khráp_ (ครับ): male respect particle
  - _khâ/khá_ (ค่ะ/คะ): female respect particle (statement/question)
- Royal language (**Ratchasap**, ราชาศัพท์) uses completely different vocabulary for monarchy
- Buddhist monastic language distinct from secular speech

**Citation Code**: {iwasaki-ingkaphirom-2005}

**Relevance**: Thai Bible translation requires gender-appropriate particles for all speakers. Royal language consideration for passages with kings and authority figures.

**Source**: Referenced in https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thai_honorifics

**Publication**: University of California Publications in Linguistics, Vol. 52. Berkeley: University of California Press.

**Key Findings**:

### 1.6 South Asian Languages

#### Masica, Colin P. (1991). _The Indo-Aryan Languages_

**Publication**: Cambridge Language Surveys. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.

**Key Findings**:
- T/V distinction widespread across Indo-Aryan family
- **Hindi-Urdu three-level system**:
  - _āp_ (आप/آپ): formal/respectful
  - _tum_ (तुम/تم): informal
  - _tū_ (तू/تو): intimate/very informal
- Verb conjugations vary by pronoun choice
- Honorific plural: singular referents take plural agreement for respect
- Caste/class historical influence on register systems

**Citation Code**: {masica-1991}

**Relevance**: Hindi Bible translation requires three-way pronoun choice. _Āp_ for God, but Jesus-disciple relationship could be _tum_ (teaching) or _āp_ (respect).

### 1.7 Tagalog/Filipino System

#### Schachter, Paul & Otanes, Fe T. (1972). _Tagalog Reference Grammar_

**Publication**: Berkeley: University of California Press.

**Key Findings**:
- **Po/opo particle system**: respect particles obligatory with elders and authority figures
  - _po_: respect particle added to sentences
  - _opo_: yes + respect particle
  - _ho_: regional variant, slightly less formal
- Focus system interacts with politeness: formal vs. informal focus constructions
- Kinship terms extended to non-kin for respect: _Tita_ (aunt), _Tito_ (uncle)
- Position of _po_ flexible: after first word, after verb, or sentence-final

**Citation Code**: {schachter-otanes-1972}

**Relevance**: Tagalog Bible translation requires _po_ when addressing God, authority figures, and elders. Particles are grammatically optional but socially obligatory.

### 1.8 Javanese Speech Levels

#### Poedjosoedarmo, Soepomo (1968). "Javanese Speech Levels"

**Publication**: _Indonesia_ 6: 54-81. Cornell Southeast Asia Program.

**Key Findings**:
- **Three main levels**: Ngoko (low), Madya (middle), Krama (high)
- **Krama subdivisions**:
  - Krama inggil (high krama): elevating others
  - Krama andhap (low krama): humbling self
- Vocabulary replacement, not just grammatical marking
- Different lexemes for same concepts across levels: _mangan_ (ngoko) vs. _dhahar_ (krama) 'eat'
- Social status, age, familiarity determine level choice
- Code-switching between levels within conversations

**Citation Code**: {poedjosoedarmo-1968}

**Relevance**: Javanese represents extreme case of speech level system with lexical replacement. Bible translation requires wholesale vocabulary changes based on speaker-listener relationships.

### 1.11 Biblical Languages

#### Waltke, Bruce K. & O'Connor, M. (1990). _An Introduction to Biblical Hebrew Syntax_

**Publication**: Winona Lake, IN: Eisenbrauns.

**Key Findings**:
- **Hebrew lacks formal/informal pronoun distinction**
- Second person pronouns: _attâ_ (you masculine singular), _att_ (you feminine singular), _attem_ (you masculine plural), _atten_ (you feminine plural)
- Number distinction (singular/plural) exists but NOT formality distinction
- Respect conveyed through titles (_Adonai_ 'Lord', _Rabbi_ 'Master'), not pronouns
- Biblical Hebrew style is direct, not using extensive honorific indirection

**Citation Code**: {waltke-oconnor-1990}

**URL**: https://wals.info/chapter/45

### 2.3 Cross-Database Insights

**Implicational Universals** (from multiple sources):
- If a language has multiple politeness levels (Japanese, Korean, Javanese), it WILL have binary distinctions embedded within
- If a language marks honorifics on pronouns, it likely marks them on verbs (e.g., Korean -si-)
- If a language has speaker-referent honorifics (humble/honorific), it WILL have speaker-addressee honorifics (Comrie's hierarchy)
- If a language uses pronoun avoidance for extreme respect, it has a pronoun-based system at lower formality levels

**Language Family Patterns**:
- **Indo-European**: T/V distinction common in European branches (Romance, Germanic, Slavic), absent in Insular Celtic and English
- **Altaic** (controversial grouping): Japanese and Korean have elaborate systems
- **Austronesian**: Variable (Tagalog/Filipino has particles, Indonesian moderate, Malay limited)
- **Sino-Tibetan**: Chinese moderate system, Tibetan more elaborate
- **Niger-Congo**: Generally absent except Swahili borrowings
- **Afro-Asiatic**: Arabic moderate through vocabulary and titles

## 3. Translation Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Japanese Bible Translation (Shinkaiyaku Seisho 2017)

**Language**: Japanese (jpn)
**Translation**: 新改訳聖書 2017 (New Japanese Bible 2017)
**Honorific System**: Three-level keigo (sonkeigo/kenjōgo/teineigo)

#### Key Translation Decisions

**1. Addressing God in Prayer**
- **Source** (Matthew 6:9): "Our Father in heaven" (no honorific marking in Greek)
- **Japanese Translation**: 天の父よ (_ten no chichi yo_)
  - Uses teineigo (polite -desu/-masu forms) throughout prayer
  - Does NOT use highest sonkeigo for God in prayer
  - Theological rationale: Intimacy as Father, not distant transcendence
  - Balances respect (teineigo) with relationship (not extreme honorific distance)

**2. Jesus Speaking to Disciples**
- **Source** (John 13:13-14): "You call me Teacher and Lord, and you are right..."
- **Japanese Translation**:
  - Jesus uses teineigo (polite forms with -desu/-masu) when teaching disciples
  - Does NOT use casual (kudaketa) forms despite intimate relationship
  - Creates respectful but not distant teacher-student dynamic
  - Disciples address Jesus with sonkeigo (respectful forms)
  - Asymmetric: Jesus → disciples (teineigo), disciples → Jesus (sonkeigo)

**3. Jesus Confronting Pharisees**
- **Source** (Matthew 23): "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites!"
- **Japanese Translation**:
  - Maintains teineigo even in confrontation
  - Rebuke conveyed through vocabulary ("hypocrites") not register lowering
  - Does NOT shift to casual/rude forms (kudaketa)
  - Theological rationale: Jesus maintains dignity even in conflict

**4. Parables with Social Hierarchy**
- **Source** (Luke 15:11-32): Prodigal Son parable
- **Japanese Translation**:
  - Father speaks to sons: teineigo (polite, paternal but respectful)
  - Sons speak to father: sonkeigo (respectful, filial)
  - Elder son to servants: casual forms (social superiority)
  - Servants to master: humble kenjōgo

#### Challenges Encountered

- **Gender-neutral Greek** → Japanese requires male/female speech patterns
- **Plural "we" ambiguity** → Japanese context-dependent pronoun choices
- **Imperative softening** → Direct commands rendered as polite requests in high-register contexts
- **Honorific inflation** → Risk of over-formalizing, creating distance from modern readers

#### Scholarly Analysis

Referenced study: "From Keigo 'Honorifics' to Keii-Hyougen 'Respect Expressions': Linguistic Ideologies of Japanese Honorifics" demonstrates that translators face challenge that "the original languages of the Bible do not have much to say about honorifics, and as such, translators are faced with difficult decisions, as the language may require every utterance to be marked as either 'polite' or 'casual' to some degree."

### Case Study 2: Korean Bible Translation (Addressing Jesus and God)

**Language**: Korean (kor)
**Translation Focus**: Ross Translation (first Korean Bible) and modern versions
**Honorific System**: Jondaemal (존댓말) vs. banmal (반말), seven historical speech levels

#### Key Translation Decisions

**1. Addressing God (Lord's Prayer)**
- **Source** (Matthew 6:9): "Our Father" (_Pater hēmōn_ in Greek - no honorific marking)
- **Korean Translation Strategy**:
  - Every "thy" translated as "Father's" (indirect address mode)
  - Avoids direct second-person pronoun
  - **Greek** _sou_ (σου, "your") → **Korean** "아바님" (_ah-bah-nyim_, honorific "father")
  - **Term for God**: 하나님 (_ha-na-nim_) or 하느님 (_ha-neu-nim_) - both honorific constructions
  - Uses highest jondaemal forms throughout

**2. Disciples Addressing Jesus**
- **Translation Strategy**:
  - Indirect address modes: "Lord" or "Teacher" instead of direct "you"
  - When pronoun necessary: highest respect forms
  - Verb endings: -습니다 (-seumnida, formal polite)
  - Honorific suffix: -시- (-si-) on verbs for Jesus as subject
  - Example: "Jesus speaks" → 예수님이 말씀하시다 (_Ye-su-nim-i mal-sseum-ha-si-da_)
    - -님 (-nim): honorific suffix on name
    - 말씀 (malsseum): honorific word for "speak" (vs. 말 _mal_)
    - -시- (-si-): subject honorific marker
    - -다 (-da): formal ending

**3. Social Hierarchy in Narratives**
- Masters to servants: lower speech level (haeyo-che or hae-che)
- Servants to masters: highest jondaemal (hasipsio-che)
- Elders to youth: medium levels
- Youth to elders: respectful jondaemal
- Jesus to crowds: teaching register (haeyo-che, accessible but respectful)

#### Cultural Context

**Korean Confucian Values**:
- Age hierarchy strictly encoded
- Respect for parents, teachers, elders grammatically obligatory
- Social status (education, position) affects speech level choice
- Korean translators placed "greater importance on the form of address than did the Chinese"

#### Theological Implications

- **Intimacy vs. Reverence**: Korean chose reverence (high honorifics) for God
- Contrasts with German Lutheran tradition (familiar _du_ for God)
- Reflects Korean cultural prioritization of hierarchical respect
- Modern debate: Should younger generation use slightly lower forms for accessibility?

**Citation Code**: {korean-case-study}

**Sources**:
- https://www.bskorea.or.kr/data/pdf/18-06 Reflections on the First Translation of Korean Bible (Hwan Jin Yi).pdf
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible_translations_into_Korean
- {wendland-2010} "Showing Respect in Bible Translation"

### Case Study 3: French Bible Translation (Tu vs. Vous for God)

**Language**: French (fra)
**Translation Focus**: Louis Segond and modern versions
**Honorific System**: T/V distinction (_tu_ vs. _vous_)

#### Historical Shift

**Traditional Practice** (pre-20th century):
- **Vous** used for God in prayers and biblical address
- Rationale: Reverence, formality, transcendence
- God as distant sovereign requiring respectful distance

**Modern Practice** (20th-21st century):
- **Tu** now standard in Catholic and Protestant prayers
- Rationale: Intimacy, familial relationship, God as Father
- Reflects theological shift emphasizing personal relationship with God

#### Specific Translation Examples

**Lord's Prayer**:
- **Traditional**: "Notre Père qui es aux cieux, que ton nom soit sanctifié..." (vous forms)
- **Modern** (Louis Segond 1910, La Bible Segond 21): "Notre Père qui es aux cieux, que **ton** nom soit sanctifié..." (tu forms)

**God Addressing Jesus** (Mark 1:11):
- "Tu es mon Fils bien-aimé" (You[informal] are my beloved Son)
- Uses tu to show intimate Father-Son relationship

**Jesus Addressing Disciples**:
- Generally uses _tu_ (informal) when teaching, showing closeness
- Exception: Formal contexts may use _vous_ for rhetorical effect

**Jesus Addressing Pharisees**:
- Varies by translation:
  - Some use _vous_ (maintaining social formality despite confrontation)
  - Some use _tu_ (sovereign disregard for their claimed status)
  - Translation choice affects tone of rebuke

#### Theological Debate

**Arguments for Tu (Informal)**:
- New Testament teaching: God as "Abba" (Daddy) - intimate term
- Jesus models intimate prayer relationship
- Emphasizes accessibility of God to believers
- Reflects post-Vatican II Catholic theology

**Arguments for Vous (Formal)**:
- Maintains reverence and awe for divine majesty
- Traditional liturgical language preserved
- Distinguishes sacred register from everyday speech
- Some older Catholics and traditionalists prefer

**Current Consensus**:
- Tu is now standard in both Catholic and Protestant French
- Vous considered archaic for God-address
- Exception: Some traditionalist communities maintain vous

**Citation Code**: {french-case-study}

**Sources**:
- https://proz.com/kudoz/french/religion/3410111-\"tu\"-or-\"vous\"-to-address-god.html
- https://www.quora.com/Shall-we-use-tu-or-vous-to-address-God-in-the-French-language
- {wendland-2010} discusses French alongside other European languages

### Cross-Case Study Insights

#### Universal Challenges

1. **Source Language Neutrality**: Hebrew, Greek, and English lack honorific systems, requiring target language translators to make non-encoded decisions

2. **Theology Influences Linguistics**: Honorific choices reflect theological positions:
   - Transcendence theology → formal/high honorifics
   - Immanence theology → informal/intimate forms
   - Incarnational theology → balanced approach

3. **Cultural Appropriateness**: No universal solution; each language-culture must determine appropriate honorific strategy

4. **Asymmetric Relationships**: God-human, Jesus-disciple, master-servant relationships require asymmetric honorific marking in most systems

5. **Register Consistency**: Maintaining consistent register throughout translation while adapting to different contexts is challenging

#### Denominational Patterns

- **Lutheran/Protestant**: Tend toward intimate forms (tu, du) emphasizing personal relationship
- **Catholic/Orthodox**: More variable; traditional reverence vs. modern accessibility
- **Evangelical**: Often prefer accessible, less formal registers for evangelistic purposes
- **Liturgical traditions**: Maintain higher registers for sacred/liturgical contexts

#### Genesis 1:26 - "Let Us Make Man"

**Hebrew**: נַעֲשֶׂה אָדָם בְּצַלְמֵנוּ (_na'aseh adam betsalmenu_) - "Let us make man in our image"

**Translation Implications**:
- Japanese: God uses teineigo (polite) even speaking within Trinity
- Korean: Highest honorifics when God addresses other divine persons
- French/Spanish: Plural _nous_ (neutral, no honorific load)

**Theological Stakes**: HIGH - affects Trinitarian doctrine

#### Matthew 13:55 - Jesus's Brothers

**Greek**: οὐχ οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ τοῦ τέκτονος υἱός; οὐχ ἡ μήτηρ αὐτοῦ λέγεται Μαριὰμ καὶ οἱ ἀδελφοὶ αὐτοῦ Ἰάκωβος καὶ Ἰωσὴφ καὶ Σίμων καὶ Ἰούδας; (_ouch houtos estin ho tou tektonos huios? ouch hē mētēr autou legetai Mariam kai hoi adelphoi autou Iakōbos kai Iōsēph kai Simōn kai Ioudas?_) - "Isn't this the carpenter's son? Isn't his mother's name Mary, and aren't his brothers James, Joseph, Simon and Judas?"

**Honorific Issues (Bantu languages)**:
- Must specify if James, Joseph, Simon, Judas are older or younger than Jesus
- **Theological implications**:
  - If older brothers: Why is Jesus family head? (Cultural challenge)
  - If younger brothers: Fits Jesus's primacy
- **Catholic tradition**: "Brothers" = cousins (preserving Mary's perpetual virginity)
- **Protestant tradition**: "Brothers" = younger half-brothers (Joseph's sons from Mary after Jesus)

**Translation Patterns**:
- Age-neutral languages: No problem
- Age-specific languages: Must make decision or use circumlocution
- Some translations use footnote explaining both possibilities

**Theological Stakes**: MEDIUM - Protestant-Catholic interpretation differences; Jesus's family status

**Key Verse**: Matthew 13:55

---

### 4.6 God Speaking to Humans

#### Genesis 12:1 - Call of Abraham

**Hebrew**: God commands Abram to leave his country

**Honorific Issues**:
- God → human: Familiar or formal?
- Command form: Direct or softened?
- Most traditions: God uses direct, authoritative speech (not softened politeness)

**Translation Patterns**:
- Languages with honorific systems generally do NOT have God use humble/deferential forms
- God may use mid-range register (accessible) or formal register (majesty)
- Rarely uses lowest familiar forms (would suggest disrespect)

**Theological Stakes**: MEDIUM - divine authority and accessibility

#### 1 Samuel 3:10 - Call of Samuel

**Hebrew**: וַיָּבֹא יְהוָה וַיִּתְיַצַּב וַיִּקְרָא כְפַעַם־בְּפַעַם שְׁמוּאֵל שְׁמוּאֵל (_vayyavo' YHWH vayyityatsav vayyiqra' kefa'am-befa'am Shemu'el Shemu'el_) - "The LORD came and stood there, calling as at the other times, 'Samuel! Samuel!'"

**Samuel's response**: דַּבֵּר כִּי שֹׁמֵעַ עַבְדֶּךָ (_dabber ki shomea' avdekha_) - "Speak, for your servant is listening"

**Honorific Issues**:
- Samuel's self-reference: עַבְדֶּךָ (_avdekha_, "your servant") - humble form
- Target languages should use humble self-reference forms (Japanese kenjōgo, Korean humble pronouns)
- God's address: Direct, no softening

**Translation Patterns**:
- Samuel → God: Highest humble/respectful forms
- God → Samuel: Direct, accessible forms

**Theological Stakes**: LOW-MEDIUM - models appropriate human response to God

**Key Verse**: 1 Samuel 3:10

**Honorific Issues**:

- Balance between liturgical dignity and personal devotion

**Theological Stakes**: MEDIUM - worship and liturgical appropriateness

## 5. Summary of Honorific Linguistic Structures

### 5.1 Mechanisms of Honorific Encoding

Languages encode honorifics through multiple linguistic levels:

**1. Pronouns** (T/V distinction)
- French: tu/vous
- Spanish: tú/usted
- German: du/Sie
- Russian: ты/вы
- Hindi: tū/tum/āp

**2. Verbal Morphology**
- Korean: -시- suffix for subject honorific
- Japanese: -masu/-desu endings (teineigo)
- Hindi: verb conjugation paradigms by respect level

**3. Lexical Replacement** (suppletive forms)
- Japanese: iku/irassharu/mairu (go: neutral/honorific/humble)
- Japanese: taberu/meshiagaru/itadaku (eat: neutral/honorific/humble)
- Korean: meokda/dŭsida (eat: plain/honorific)
- Javanese: complete vocabulary replacement across ngoko/madya/krama

**4. Particles**
- Thai: khráp (ครับ) male, khâ/khá (ค่ะ/คะ) female
- Tagalog: po/opo
- Hindi: -jī suffix

**5. Pronoun Avoidance**
- Korean: use titles instead of pronouns (선생님, _seon-saeng-nim_ "teacher")
- Japanese: use names + -san/-sama instead of "you"
- Vietnamese: use kinship terms as pronouns

**6. Indirectness**
- Questions instead of commands
- Conditional mood ("Could you..." vs. "Do this")
- Passive constructions to avoid direct agent

---

### 5.2 Honorific Axes (Comrie 1976)

**Speaker-Addressee** (most common):
- T/V pronouns (European languages)
- Speech levels (Japanese, Korean, Javanese)
- Particles (Thai, Tagalog)

**Speaker-Referent** (East Asian):
- Japanese sonkeigo (respect toward person mentioned)
- Japanese kenjōgo (humble forms when speaking of self/in-group)
- Korean -시- honorific marker on verbs for respected subjects

**Speaker-Bystander** (rare):
- Register adjustment when third parties overhear
- More formal in public than private settings

---

### 5.3 Social Deixis

**Definition**: Honorifics are form of social deixis - linguistic elements that encode social relationships and relative status of discourse participants.

**Dimensions**:
- **Power**: Vertical, asymmetric (superior-subordinate)
- **Solidarity**: Horizontal, symmetric (in-group vs. out-group)
- **Formality**: Situational (casual vs. formal context)

**Person Deixis Integration**:
- First person: humble forms (Japanese kenjōgo)
- Second person: respectful/casual forms (T/V)
- Third person: honorific/neutral (Japanese sonkeigo)

## Appendix: Top 10 Research Findings (Executive Summary)

### 1. Source Language Neutrality Creates Translation Challenge
**Finding**: Biblical Hebrew and Greek lack honorific systems, forcing translators to make non-encoded decisions about formality and respect marking in target languages that grammatically require honorifics.

**Impact**: ~25% of world's languages (per WALS Feature 45) require honorific marking, creating systematic translation challenge for Bible translation into Japanese, Korean, Thai, Vietnamese, Hindi, Javanese, and many others.

---

### 2. Brown & Gilman's Power-Solidarity Framework (1960) Remains Foundational
**Finding**: The dimensions of power (vertical, asymmetric) and solidarity (horizontal, symmetric) explain T/V pronoun choices across European languages and extend to more complex Asian systems.

**Impact**: Provides theoretical framework for analyzing Jesus-disciple (solidarity + power), Jesus-Pharisee (power without solidarity), and human-God (power + transcendence/intimacy variation) relationships.

---

### 3. Brown & Levinson's Face Theory (1987) Explains Politeness Strategies
**Finding**: Positive face (desire for approval) vs. negative face (desire for autonomy) motivates politeness strategies: bald on-record, positive politeness, negative politeness, and off-record indirectness.

**Impact**: Honorifics serve primarily as negative politeness strategies (showing deference). Bible translation must consider face-management in different contexts: prayer (managing God's face), teaching (managing disciples' face), confrontation (face-threatening acts toward Pharisees).

---

### 4. Ide's Discernment (Wakimae) Theory (1989) Challenges Western Universalism
**Finding**: Japanese and Korean politeness operates primarily through **discernment** (obligatory, relationship-based) rather than **volition** (strategic, choice-based) emphasized in Western cultures.

**Impact**: Asian Bible translations cannot treat honorifics as optional stylistic choices. Every utterance MUST be marked for social relationships. Algorithm must systematically encode speaker-listener relationships for every verse.

---

### 5. Three-Dimensional Honorific Axes (Comrie 1976)
**Finding**: Languages mark honorifics along three axes:
1. Speaker-addressee (formality toward listener)
2. Speaker-referent (respect toward person mentioned)
3. Speaker-bystander (audience design)

**Impact**: Japanese and Korean Bible translations must consider not just addressing God/Jesus (speaker-addressee) but also referring to them in third person (speaker-referent). Jesus uses humble forms about himself (kenjōgo) but receives respectful forms (sonkeigo) from others.

---

### 6. Typological Distribution: Simple to Complex Continuum
**Finding**: Honorific complexity ranges from:
- **None**: English, Swahili, Finnish (no grammatical marking)
- **Binary**: European T/V (formal/informal)
- **Three-level**: Hindi (tū/tum/āp), French (historical tu/vous/vous-plural)
- **Multi-level**: Korean (7 historical levels, 4 modern), Javanese (ngoko/madya/krama with subdivisions), Thai (extensive pronoun inventory)
- **Comprehensive**: Japanese (keigo system integrating pronouns, verbs, vocabulary across three dimensions)

**Impact**: TBTA algorithm must scale from simple binary feature to complex multi-dimensional system depending on target language.

---

### 7. WALS Feature 45: Only 25% of Languages Have Politeness Distinctions in Pronouns
**Finding**: Of 207 languages sampled:
- 75% have NO politeness distinctions in pronouns
- Of the 25% that do: 66% have binary distinction, 10% have multiple levels, 20% use pronoun avoidance

**Geographic patterns**: Common in Europe, East/Southeast Asia, South Asia; absent in most of Africa, Americas, Oceania

**Impact**: Honorific marking is NOT universal. Algorithm must first identify IF target language requires honorific marking, then determine type and complexity.

---

### 8. Theological Interpretation Embedded in Honorific Choices
**Finding**: Honorific decisions reflect theological positions:
- **Transcendence theology** → Formal/high honorifics for God (traditional French _vous_, Korean highest _jondaemal_)
- **Immanence/intimacy theology** → Informal/familiar forms (modern French _tu_, German Lutheran _du_)
- **Christ's dual nature** → Jesus as human (accessible teineigo in Japanese) but divine (receives sonkeigo from others)

**Impact**: Algorithm should allow denominational variation in honorific choices based on theological preference. No single "correct" solution; cultural and theological appropriateness key.

---

### 9. Bantu Age-Based Kinship Creates Translation Decisions
**Finding**: Bantu languages (Swahili, Zulu, Xhosa, etc.) grammatically MUST specify older vs. younger in kinship terms. Greek ἀδελφός (_adelphos_ 'brother') is age-neutral.

**Impact**: Passages like Matthew 4:18 (Peter and Andrew), Matthew 13:55 (Jesus's brothers) require translators to research or infer age relationships not encoded in source text. Affects cultural understanding and Jesus's family status.

---

### 10. Japanese Case Study: Shinkaiyaku Seisho 2017 Translation Strategy
**Finding**: Japanese Bible translation uses:
- **God**: Teineigo (polite -masu/-desu forms), NOT highest sonkeigo (balances intimacy with respect)
- **Jesus → disciples**: Teineigo (teaching register, respectful but accessible)
- **Disciples → Jesus**: Sonkeigo (respectful, acknowledging authority)
- **Jesus → Pharisees**: Maintains teineigo even in confrontation (dignified rebuke through vocabulary, not register lowering)

**Impact**: Demonstrates successful balance between:
1. Cultural appropriateness (Japanese respect requirements)
2. Theological accuracy (relationships encoded appropriately)
3. Accessibility (not over-formalizing, creating distance from modern readers)

Model for other complex honorific languages.
