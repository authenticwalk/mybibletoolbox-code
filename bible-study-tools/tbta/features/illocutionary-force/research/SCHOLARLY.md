## Executive Summary

Illocutionary force identifies the speech act function of an utterance—whether it performs as a directive (command, request), interrogative (question), assertive (statement, declaration), commissive (promise, vow), or expressive (blessing, curse). Founded by J.L. Austin and systematized by John Searle, speech act theory distinguishes between locutionary acts (propositional content), illocutionary acts (communicative intention), and perlocutionary acts (effects on hearers). Biblical languages encode illocutionary force through dedicated morphology (Hebrew imperatives, jussives, cohortatives; Greek imperatives, subjunctives, interrogatives) and discourse markers (particles like נָא for politeness, question particles). Translation challenges arise from cross-linguistic variation: languages differ in whether they mark illocutionary force morphologically, syntactically, or through intonation alone. Key biblical passages include the Great Commission (Matthew 28:19—imperative vs. attendant circumstance debate), Decalogue (Exodus 20—"ten words" vs. "ten commandments"), Genesis 22 (divine imperatives with heightened force), and prophetic rhetorical questions (Jonah 4:11). Theologically, accurate rendering of illocutionary force prevents doctrinal error (e.g., distinguishing divine commands from human requests, preserving covenantal commissives). Cross-linguistic typology shows five patterns for imperatives (WALS 70A) and multiple strategies for polar questions (WALS 116A). Modern pragmatic approaches (Relevance Theory, Gricean implicature) help translators identify indirect speech acts where surface form differs from communicative intention. This research synthesizes linguistic typology, biblical exegesis, and translation theory to guide accurate TBTA annotation.

### Top 10 Key Findings

1. **Foundational Theory**: J.L. Austin's "How to Do Things with Words" (1955/1962) introduced the locutionary-illocutionary-perlocutionary distinction; John Searle (1969, 1975) systematized illocutionary force into five categories: assertives, directives, commissives, expressives, and declarations.

2. **Biblical Greek Imperatives**: Daniel Wallace identifies seven uses of imperatives beyond simple commands: prohibition, request (with politeness markers), permissive, conditional, pronouncement, and stereotyped greetings. Porter's verbal aspect theory shows present vs. aorist imperatives encode imperfective vs. perfective aspect, not general vs. specific commands.

3. **Hebrew Volitional System**: Biblical Hebrew distinguishes three volitional forms by person: imperatives (2nd person), cohortatives (1st person, marked by ־הָּ), and jussives (3rd person). Waltke-O'Connor and van der Merwe document how context determines whether these function as commands, wishes, permissions, or polite requests.

4. **Translation Debate—Great Commission**: Matthew 28:19's πορευθέντες ("go") is grammatically a participle but may function imperatively through "attendant circumstance." Scholars debate whether to translate as imperative "Go!" or modal "having gone/as you go," with significant missiological implications.

5. **WALS Typology—Imperatives (Feature 70A)**: Van der Auwera et al. identify five cross-linguistic patterns: (1) both singular and plural dedicated forms, (2) singular only, (3) plural only, (4) number-neutral, (5) no dedicated imperative. Most languages worldwide have dedicated 2nd person imperative morphology.

6. **WALS Typology—Polar Questions (Feature 116A)**: Dryer documents four main strategies for yes/no questions: (1) question particles (most common), (2) interrogative verbal morphology, (3) both particles and morphology, (4) word order change. Understanding source language strategy aids translation into target languages with different systems.

7. **Rhetorical Questions in Biblical Hebrew**: Scholarly analysis shows Biblical Hebrew has unusually high frequency of rhetorical questions compared to everyday speech. These function as emphatic assertions with implied answers, not genuine information-seeking. Translation must preserve illocutionary force (assertion) while maintaining interrogative surface form.

8. **Performative Utterances**: Dobbs-Allsopp and Rogland research on Semitic languages shows blessings and curses function as performative speech acts—utterances that accomplish what they say. Hebrew distinguishes אָלָה (malediction/pronouncement) from אָרָר (decree/operational curse), with different illocutionary forces requiring distinct translation strategies.

9. **Indirect Speech Acts and Politeness**: Biblical Hebrew particle נָא softens imperatives into polite requests (Genesis 22:2: "please take your son"). Searle's theory explains why indirect speech acts are only partly conventionalized, causing translation challenges when languages conventionalize different forms.

10. **Theological Application**: Wolterstorff's "Divine Discourse" and Vanhoozer's speech-act hermeneutics argue that understanding illocutionary force is essential for interpreting what God is doing through Scripture (commanding, promising, warning), not just what God is saying propositionally. Misidentifying illocutionary force leads to misapplication.

## 1. Foundational Scholarly Sources

### 1.1 Speech Act Theory—Core Works

#### Austin, J.L. (1955/1962). *How to Do Things with Words*

- **Source**: [J.L. Austin - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._L._Austin), [Speech Acts (Stanford Encyclopedia)](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/speech-acts/)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Introduced distinction between **performative utterances** (which do actions, like "I pronounce you married") and **constative utterances** (which describe facts)
  - Developed three-part analysis: **locutionary act** (producing meaningful expression), **illocutionary act** (performing an action such as asserting, questioning, promising), **perlocutionary act** (bringing about effects on addressee)
  - Identified **felicity conditions**: performatives can be "infelicitous" (unsuccessful) rather than false when contextual conditions aren't met
- **Citation Code**: {austin-1962-htdw}
- **Relevance**: Foundational framework for understanding how biblical utterances *do* things (command, bless, promise) beyond merely stating propositions. Essential for distinguishing divine decrees from human reports.

#### Searle, John R. (1969). *Speech Acts: An Essay in the Philosophy of Language*

- **Source**: [Speech Acts (Stanford Encyclopedia)](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/speech-acts/), [J.L. Austin and John Searle on Speech Act Theory](https://www.thecollector.com/speech-act-theory-austin-and-searle/)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Systematized Austin's theory with explicit rules for performing illocutionary acts
  - Proposed **taxonomy of illocutionary acts** based on illocutionary point and direction of fit:
    1. **Assertives**: commit speaker to truth of proposition (statements, assertions)
    2. **Directives**: attempt to get hearer to do something (commands, requests, questions)
    3. **Commissives**: commit speaker to future action (promises, vows, oaths)
    4. **Expressives**: express psychological states (thanks, apologies, blessings)
    5. **Declarations**: bring about correspondence between propositional content and reality through the utterance itself (pronouncements, decrees)
  - Distinguished **direct** vs. **indirect speech acts**: e.g., "Can you pass the salt?" is grammatically a question (direct) but functions as a request (indirect)
- **Citation Code**: {searle-1969-sa}
- **Relevance**: Provides systematic categories for classifying biblical speech acts. Explains how a single utterance can have multiple illocutionary forces (literal question + indirect request).

#### Searle, John R. (1975). "Indirect Speech Acts"

- **Source**: [Chapter 2: Indirect Speech Acts](https://danielwharris.com/teaching/spring16/readings/SearleIndirect.pdf)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Explained how speakers mean more than they literally say through calculable inference
  - Indirect speech acts arise from Gricean conversational maxims plus mutual knowledge
  - Languages conventionalize different indirect forms: e.g., English "Can you X?" for requests is conventional, but cross-linguistic variation exists
- **Citation Code**: {searle-1975-isa}
- **Relevance**: Critical for translating biblical politeness strategies and understanding when biblical imperatives function as strong commands vs. polite requests.

### 1.2 Linguistic Semantics and Pragmatics

#### Lyons, John (1977). *Semantics* (Vol. 2, Chapters 16-17)

- **Source**: [Mood and illocutionary force (Chapter 16)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/semantics/mood-and-illocutionary-force/DC8EC571ABB94D0C2FC37E5602995D20), [Speech acts and illocutionary force (Chapter 8)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/linguistic-semantics/speech-acts-and-illocutionary-force/1467AE642F972A826D4AD0A9A33C1ED6)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Chapter 16 covers mood and illocutionary force, addressing speech-acts, commands/requests/demands, questions, negation
  - Chapter 17 covers modality: necessity/possibility, epistemic modality, deontic modality, obligation/permission/prohibition
  - Distinguished grammatical mood (morphological category) from illocutionary force (pragmatic function)
- **Citation Code**: {lyons-1977-semantics}
- **Relevance**: Clarifies that grammatical mood (imperative, subjunctive) ≠ illocutionary force (command, request). Hebrew/Greek modal forms can have multiple illocutionary uses.

#### Grice, H.P. (1975). "Logic and Conversation"

- **Source**: [Implicature (Stanford Encyclopedia)](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/implicature/), [Grice's theory of conversational implicature](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/grices-theory-of-conversational-implicature/13327978)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Proposed **Cooperative Principle** with four maxims: Quality (truthfulness), Quantity (appropriate information), Relation (relevance), Manner (clarity)
  - **Conversational implicature**: what speaker means beyond literal meaning, calculable from maxims + context
  - Explains indirect speech acts: violating Manner maxim to achieve politeness
- **Citation Code**: {grice-1975-logic}
- **Relevance**: Helps identify when biblical authors convey meaning beyond literal words. Explains how rhetorical questions implicate assertions without stating them directly.

#### Sperber, Dan & Wilson, Deirdre (1986/1995). *Relevance: Communication and Cognition*

- **Source**: [Relevance Theory in biblical interpretation](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/people-of-god-in-the-apocalypse/relevance-theory-in-biblical-interpretation/9B2B3401154E7E77CCB6B8F72E9B974B), [Relevance Theory (Oxford Handbook)](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/28055/chapter/211998868)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Proposes **Relevance Theory**: communication aims at optimal relevance (maximal cognitive effects for minimal processing effort)
  - Provides rigorous account of inference in communication, including figures of speech, irony, hyperbole
  - Applied to biblical interpretation: explains how "underspecified biblical text expressing an abstract norm was enriched pragmatically"
- **Citation Code**: {sperber-wilson-1995-relevance}
- **Relevance**: Offers cognitive framework for understanding how translators infer intended illocutionary force from context when grammatical markers are ambiguous.

### 1.3 Linguistic Typology

#### Comrie, Bernard (1981). *Language Universals and Linguistic Typology: Syntax and Morphology*

- **Source**: [Language Universals and Linguistic Typology](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/L/bo24426144.html)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Leading introductory account of linguistic typology

  - Distinguishes morphological forms from pragmatic functions in context
- **Citation Code**: {waltke-oconnor-1990-bhsyn}
- **Relevance**: Standard reference for understanding how Hebrew volitional forms (imperative, jussive, cohortative) encode different illocutionary forces depending on context.

#### Van der Merwe, Christo H.J., Naudé, Jacobus A., & Kroeze, Jan H. (1999/2017). *A Biblical Hebrew Reference Grammar* (2nd ed.)

- **Source**: [Biblical Hebrew Reference Grammar (Archive.org)](https://archive.org/details/biblicalhebrewre0000vand)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Covers morphology, syntax, semantics, and pragmatics of Biblical Hebrew
  - Discusses volitional mood in §19.4 and §45.5.i

  - Integrates traditional knowledge with recent general linguistics
- **Citation Code**: {vandermerwe-2017-bhrg}
- **Relevance**: Shows how pragmatic particles modify illocutionary force from command to request. Essential for understanding politeness strategies in Hebrew imperatives.

### 2.2 Imperatives and Volitional Forms

#### Ritter, Elizabeth. "Imperatives and prohibitives in Biblical Hebrew"

- **Source**: [Imperatives and prohibitives in Biblical Hebrew](https://lingpapers.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2024/11/UBCOPL_Hotzes_Volume_Ritter.pdf)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Imperative verbs check a feature in higher functional head (Mood, C, or Force)
  - Imperative force not determined solely by form—syntax and discourse markers govern function
  - כֹּה followed by imperfect may function as imperative
  - וְעַתָּה introduces commands through jussive or imperfect forms
  - Third-person jussives function as indirect imperatives
- **Citation Code**: {ritter-2024-imperatives}
- **Relevance**: Demonstrates that Hebrew illocutionary force depends on discourse markers and syntax, not just morphology. Critical for accurate TBTA annotation.

#### "Imperative Forms in Biblical Hebrew: Commanding with Precision"

- **Source**: [Imperative Forms in Biblical Hebrew](https://biblicalhebrew.org/imperative-forms-in-biblical-hebrew-commanding-with-precision.aspx)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Imperative can function as request when softened by נָא ("please"), as in Genesis 22:2
  - Particle נָא "presents a command or direction as a request (often translatable with 'please') so as to provide redress"
  - Deuteronomy 6:4-5 shows Perfect with waw consecutive conveying same force as preceding imperative
- **Citation Code**: {biblicalhebrew-imperative}
- **Relevance**: Shows how particles modify illocutionary force. Essential for distinguishing strong commands from polite requests in translation.

#### "Cohortatives & Jussives"

- **Source**: [Hebrew Grammar - Cohortatives & Jussives](https://www.blueletterbible.org/resources/grammars/hebrew/simplified-hebrew/corhortives-and-jussives.cfm)
- **Key Findings**:
  - **Person distribution**: Imperative (2nd person only), Cohortative (1st person only), Jussive (usually 3rd person)
  - **Cohortative**: Marked by appended הָּ to 1st person imperfect; translates "Let us…" or "We should…"
  - **Jussive**: Expresses wish, polite request, or softer command; translates "let" or "may"
  - Genesis 1:3 ("let there be light") exemplifies jussive
  - Genesis 1:26 ("Let us make man") exemplifies cohortative
- **Citation Code**: {blb-cohortatives-jussives}
- **Relevance**: Three-way person-based system for encoding illocutionary force in Hebrew. Target languages without this distinction require different translation strategies.

### 2.3 Rhetorical Questions

#### "Why Does the Hebrew Bible Use So Many Rhetorical Questions? Why Not?"

- **Source**: [PDF on ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/378309958_Why_Does_the_Hebrew_Bible_Use_So_Many_Rhetorical_Questions_Why_Not)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Biblical Hebrew has unusually high frequency of rhetorical questions vs. everyday speech
  - Rhetorical questions function pragmatically as assertions with implied answers
  - "Illocutionary force is not to question" but to make emphatic assertion
  - Used to initiate conversation, test people, influence public, emphasize points, even humor
- **Citation Code**: {researchgate-rq-hebrew}
- **Relevance**: Demonstrates mismatch between grammatical form (interrogative) and illocutionary force (assertive). Translation must preserve both surface form and pragmatic function.

#### "Rhetorical Question or Assertion? The Pragmatics of [particle] in Biblical Hebrew"

- **Source**: [JANES Article PDF](https://janes.scholasticahq.com/article/2516-rhetorical-question-or-assertion-the-pragmatics-of-in-biblical-hebrew/attachment/6626.pdf)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Rhetorical question is "sentence in grammatical form of a question… Its 'illocutionary force' is not to question"
  - Functions as assertion whose content is the implied answer
  - Rhetorical question is "intensifier, deriving force from implication of obviousness"
  - Sometimes serves as mitigation (less direct than assertion)
- **Citation Code**: {janes-rq-pragmatics}
- **Relevance**: Explains dual function of rhetorical questions: intensification and mitigation. Target languages may need different strategies to preserve both functions.

### 2.4 Performative Utterances (Blessings and Curses)

#### Dobbs-Allsopp, F.W. (2004-2007). "(More) On Performatives in Semitic" *ZAH* 17-20: 36-81

- **Source**: [Referenced on Balshanut blog](https://balshanut.wordpress.com/2008/06/05/dobbs-allsopp-fw-%E2%80%9Cmore-on-performatives-in-semitic%E2%80%9D-zah-17-20-2004-2007-36-81/)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Performative utterance: "uttering of sentence does not describe or report action, but is itself part of the action"
  - Mainly part of social conventions and rituals: greetings, vows, blessings
  - No "performative perfects" in Biblical Hebrew if referring to semantic meaning of perfective form
  - If suffix conjugation used for performatives, cannot grammaticalize past tense—must be aspectual form
- **Citation Code**: {dobbs-allsopp-2007-performatives}
- **Relevance**: Clarifies that Hebrew blessings/curses are speech acts that accomplish what they declare. Translation must preserve performative function, not just descriptive meaning.

#### Rogland, Max (2001). "Performative Utterances in Classical Syriac" *JSS* 46.2: 243-250

- **Source**: [PDF on Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/492275/_Performative_Utterances_in_Classical_Syriac_Journal_of_Semitic_Studies_46_2_2001_243_250)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Performatives worked in ancient West Semitic languages and cultures
  - Focuses on Ugaritic texts with pragmatically rich contexts
  - Proposes new grammatical definition of performatives in Semitic
- **Citation Code**: {rogland-2001-performatives}
- **Relevance**: Comparative Semitic data for understanding how biblical performatives functioned. Helps distinguish illocutionary force of performative declarations from constative statements.

#### "Blessing and Cursing" (Biblical Encyclopedia)

- **Source**: [Bible Odyssey - Curse and Blessing](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/curse-and-blessing/), [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/blessing-and-cursing)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Recent scholarship: efficacy of curse understood through speech act theory
  - Hebrew אָלָה (*alah*) = curse from point of view of pronouncement as "malediction"
  - Hebrew אָרָר (*arar*) = curse as operational decree rather than prayer
  - When humans invoke: blessings/curses are prayers; when Deity pronounces: decree
  - Language attributed to God or invoking divine authority expresses blessing/cursing
- **Citation Code**: {bibleodyssey-curse-blessing}
- **Relevance**: Shows how agent (human vs. divine) changes illocutionary force from prayer to decree. Essential for translating prophetic pronouncements vs. human petitions.

## 3. Biblical Greek: Illocutionary Force Encoding

### 3.1 Reference Grammars

#### Wallace, Daniel B. (1996). *Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics: An Exegetical Syntax of the New Testament*

- **Source**: [Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics (Amazon)](https://www.amazon.com/Greek-Grammar-Beyond-Basics-Exegetical/dp/0310218950)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Imperative mood "imposes intention and volition onto action, not purely a command"
  - **Seven specific uses of imperative**:
    1. **Command**: Most common, directives differentiated by tense
    2. **Prohibition**: Negative commands, primarily present tense
    3. **Request**: Polite commands/entreaties, often to superior
    4. **Permissive**: Indicates allowance or toleration
    5. **Conditional**: Indicates condition for fulfillment
    6. **Pronouncement**: Inevitable statements as commands
    7. **Stereotyped Greetings**: Exclamatory use
  - Corrects incorrect presuppositions about imperatives and conditional sentences
- **Citation Code**: {wallace-1996-ggbb}
- **Relevance**: Shows single morphological form (imperative) has seven distinct illocutionary forces. Context determines which function applies.

#### Porter, Stanley E. (1989). *Verbal Aspect in the Greek of the New Testament*

- **Source**: [Aspect and Imperatives Once More (PDF)](http://bagl.org/files/volume7/BAGL_7-5_Porter.pdf)
- **Key Findings**:
  - NT grammars often assert perfective (aorist) imperative = specific commands, imperfective (present) = general commands
  - No fully satisfactory way to account for variation of verbal aspect in imperative mood
  - Synthetic verbal aspect provides workable model for explaining tense-forms
  - Conversation analysis approach to imperatives in Greek
- **Citation Code**: {porter-2007-aspect-imperatives}
- **Relevance**: Challenges traditional "specific vs. general command" theory. Aspect encodes viewpoint, not illocutionary force—context determines command type.

### 3.3 Hortatory Subjunctive

#### "The Hortatory Subjunctive"

- **Source**: [A Workbook of New Testament Greek](https://koineworkbook.wordpress.com/2015/06/29/the-hortatory-subjunctive/)
- **Key Findings**:
  - First person plural subjunctive as exhortation: "let us [do something]"
  - Polite way of encouraging others to join speaker in action
  - Sometimes functions as command
  - Always first person plural subjunctive mood
  - Example: Matthew 21:38 "let us kill him" (ἀποκτείνωμεν)
  - Hebrews 4:16: "Let us approach the throne with boldness" (προσερχώμεθα)
- **Citation Code**: {koineworkbook-hortatory}
- **Relevance**: Greek encodes directive illocutionary force through mood (hortatory subjunctive) distinct from imperative. Translation must preserve inclusive exhortation vs. direct command distinction.

## 4. Speech Act Theory and Biblical Interpretation

### 4.1 Theological Applications

#### Wolterstorff, Nicholas (1995). *Divine Discourse: Philosophical Reflections on the Claim that God Speaks*

- **Source**: [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/divine-discourse/82E0BB77A24320802E7F6A14B6AC78B8)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Argues philosophers mistakenly assimilated divine speech to divine revelation
  - Embraces contemporary speech-action theory for analyzing divine communication
  - Claim that God performs illocutionary actions is coherent and entails no falsehoods
  - God speaks through "double agency"—discourse through words not uttered by oneself
  - Develops theory of double-hermeneutics for interpreting what God says via text
  - Opposes Ricoeur/Derrida consensus on text interpretation
- **Citation Code**: {wolterstorff-1995-divine-discourse}
- **Relevance**: Theological framework for understanding Scripture as divine illocutionary acts. God not just revealing propositions but commanding, promising, warning through text.

#### Vanhoozer, Kevin J. (1998). *Is There a Meaning in This Text? The Bible, the Reader, and the Morality of Literary Knowledge*

  - Biblical text as communicative act: **locutions** (text), **illocutions** (author's stance: questioning, asserting, promising), **perlocutions** (goals author hopes to accomplish)
  - Links three components of speech act to Trinity:
    - Locution, illocution, perlocution
    - Creation, Incarnation/revelation, sanctification
    - Father, Son, Spirit
  - Meaning grounded in God's communicative action in creation, canon, and Christ
- **Citation Code**: {vanhoozer-1998-meaning}
- **Relevance**: Integrates speech act theory with Christian theology of Scripture. Illocutionary force = divine action through text, not just human literary intent.

  - Speech-act models applied to enquiring and believing reading
- **Citation Code**: {thiselton-1992-horizons}

## 5. Typological Databases

### 5.1 WALS (World Atlas of Language Structures)

#### WALS Feature 70A: The Morphological Imperative

- **Source**: [WALS Chapter 70](https://wals.info/chapter/70), [WALS Feature 70A](https://wals.info/feature/70A)
- **Authors**: Johan van der Auwera, Ludo Lejeune, Umarani Pappuswamy, Valentin Goussev
- **Key Findings**:
  - Maps extent to which languages have 2nd person singular/plural imperatives as dedicated morphological categories
  - **Five typological patterns**:
    1. **Both singular and plural**: Special morphology for both 2sg and 2pl imperatives (e.g., Limbu)
    2. **Singular only**: Dedicated 2sg imperative, but 2pl uses subjunctive/other form (e.g., Lingala)
    3. **Plural only**: Dedicated 2pl imperative, but 2sg uses indicative present (e.g., Latvian)
    4. **Number-neutral**: One imperative form for both singular and plural (e.g., Paiwan)
    5. **No dedicated imperative**: Uses future indicative or other form for commands (e.g., Nunggubuyu)
  - Most common worldwide: dedicated 2sg and 2pl imperatives (Type 1)
- **Citation Code**: {wals-70a-imperative}
- **Relevance**: Shows cross-linguistic variation in imperative encoding. Hebrew/Greek have Type 1 (both sg/pl); target languages may have Types 2-5, requiring different translation strategies.

#### WALS Feature 72A: Imperative-Hortative Systems

- **Source**: [WALS Chapter 72](https://wals.info/chapter/72)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Imperatives and hortatives "both have to do with expression of wish of speaker about future state of affairs"
  - Unlike optatives, they "convey appeal to addressee(s) to help make future state true"
  - Maps how languages combine imperative and hortative systems
- **Citation Code**: {wals-72a-imperative-hortative}
- **Relevance**: Hebrew cohortative (1st person) vs. imperative (2nd person) parallels cross-linguistic imperative-hortative distinction. Target languages vary in encoding this contrast.

#### WALS Feature 71A: The Prohibitive

- **Source**: [WALS Chapter 71](https://wals.info/chapter/71)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Examines negative imperatives (prohibitives)
  - Describes how languages combine imperative constructions with negative strategies
  - Cross-linguistic variation in whether prohibitive uses same form as affirmative imperative + negation
- **Citation Code**: {wals-71a-prohibitive}
- **Relevance**: Greek uses different forms for prohibitive (present imperative vs. aorist subjunctive with μή). Target languages may not distinguish, requiring translation notes.

#### WALS Feature 116A: Polar Questions

- **Source**: [WALS Chapter 116](https://wals.info/chapter/116), [WALS Feature 116A](https://wals.info/feature/116A)
- **Author**: Matthew S. Dryer
- **Key Findings**:
  - Polar questions = yes/no questions ("expected answer is equivalent of 'yes' or 'no'")
  - **Four main strategies**:
    1. **Question Particles**: Added to declarative sentence to signal question (most common worldwide)
    2. **Interrogative Verbal Morphology**: Distinct verbal inflection for questions
    3. **Both Particles and Morphology**: Either separate or co-occurring
    4. **Word Order**: Different word order from declarative (e.g., German V-initial)
  - Separate question particles more common than interrogative verb morphology
- **Citation Code**: {wals-116a-polar-questions}
- **Relevance**: Hebrew uses interrogative particles (הֲ); Greek uses particles (ἆρα, μή, οὐ) and intonation. Target languages using only intonation need explicit question markers in translation.

#### WALS Feature 93A: Position of Interrogative Phrases in Content Questions

- **Source**: [WALS Chapter 93](https://wals.info/chapter/93), [WALS Feature 93A](https://wals.info/feature/93A)
- **Key Findings**:
  - Content questions "elicit specific answer other than 'yes' or 'no'" (WH-questions)
  - Maps position of interrogative phrases: initial, non-initial, mixed
  - Different from polar questions
- **Citation Code**: {wals-93a-interrogative-position}
- **Relevance**: Hebrew/Greek allow flexible interrogative word position for rhetorical effect. Translation into languages with fixed WH-position may lose pragmatic emphasis.

## Executive Summary

Illocutionary force—the speech act performed by an utterance—is a **pragmatic universal** varying widely in how languages encode it. While all languages distinguish basic sentence types (declarative, interrogative, imperative), **encoding strategies** range from obligatory sentence-final particles (Japanese, Korean, Mandarin) to purely intonational (many African and European languages). Biblical Hebrew and Greek encode illocutionary force through **volitional verb forms** (imperative, cohortative, jussive) and **mood systems** (indicative, subjunctive, optative, imperative), providing explicit guidance often absent in English translations.

**Theological Challenges**:
- **Genesis 1:3, 26**: Jussive force (divine fiat, Trinitarian cohortative) requires precise rendering
- **Rhetorical questions**: Form-function misalignment (interrogative syntax, declarative force)
- **Indirect speech acts**: Politeness strategies (Brown & Levinson) affect command interpretation
- **Honorific systems**: East Asian languages require illocutionary force + politeness level selection

**Key Insight**: Bible translators must navigate **three levels**:
1. **Source text illocutionary force** (Hebrew/Greek grammatical encoding)
2. **Semantic intention** (what God/author meant to do with words)
3. **Target language requirements** (how that force must be expressed)

### 1.1 Austin, J. L. (1962). *How to Do Things with Words*

**Citation Code**: {austin-1962-performative}

**Key Findings**:

### 1.2 Searle, John R. (1969). *Speech Acts: An Essay in the Philosophy of Language*

**Citation Code**: {searle-1969-speech-acts}

**Key Findings**:
- Developed **taxonomy of illocutionary acts** into five categories:
  1. **Assertives (Representatives)**: Commit speaker to truth (state, claim, conclude)
  2. **Directives**: Attempts to get hearer to do something (command, request, advise)
  3. **Commissives**: Commit speaker to future action (promise, threaten, vow)
  4. **Expressives**: Express psychological state (thank, apologize, congratulate, condole)
  5. **Declarations**: Create reality by being uttered (baptize, pronounce, name)

- Introduced **illocutionary force indicating devices (IFIDs)**:
  - Word order
  - Stress and intonation
  - Punctuation
  - Mood of verb (especially imperative)
  - Performative verbs

- Proposed **F(p)** formula: Illocutionary Force (Propositional Content)
  - Same proposition p, different forces F → different speech acts
  - Example: "You will leave" (prediction) vs. "You will leave!" (command)

**Relevance for Our Algorithm**:
- TBTA's five values (Declarative, Interrogative, Imperative, Suggestive, Jussive) map roughly to Searle's taxonomy:
  - Declarative → Assertives
  - Imperative + Jussive → Directives
  - (Commissives, Expressives, Declarations not distinguished by TBTA)
- **IFIDs** = what our algorithm must detect:
  - Hebrew: cohortative morphology = IFID for jussive force
  - Greek: subjunctive mood + context = IFID for hortative force
  - Target language: must insert appropriate IFID (Japanese か, Korean -습니까, etc.)

**Source**: [Searle's Classification of Speech Acts](https://www.coli.uni-saarland.de/projects/milca/courses/dialogue/html/node66.html)

### 1.3 Searle, John R. & Vanderveken, Daniel (1985). *Foundations of Illocutionary Logic*

**Citation Code**: {searle-vanderveken-1985-logic}

**Key Findings**:
- Decomposed illocutionary force into **seven components**:
  1. **Illocutionary point**: Characteristic aim (assert truth, get hearer to act, commit to action)
  2. **Mode of achievement**: How the point is achieved
  3. **Propositional content conditions**: Constraints on proposition (e.g., promises require future action)
  4. **Preparatory conditions**: Contextual prerequisites (e.g., questions presuppose speaker doesn't know answer)
  5. **Sincerity conditions**: Speaker's psychological state (e.g., requests require desire)
  6. **Degree of strength**: Intensity of force (request < demand < order)
  7. **Degree of strength of sincerity condition**

**Relevance for Our Algorithm**:
- **Distinguishing similar forces**:
  - Request vs. Order vs. Suggestion → same illocutionary point (directive), different **degree of strength**
  - TBTA's "Suggestive" may be **weak directive** (suggestion) vs. "Imperative" (strong directive)
- **Genesis 1:3 "Let there be"**:
  - Illocutionary point: Directive (bring about state of affairs)
  - Degree of strength: **Absolute** (divine fiat, no resistance possible)
  - Mode of achievement: **Performative** (utterance itself creates)

**Source**: [Illocutionary Force Indicating Devices](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/~haroldfs/dravling/illocutionary.html)

### 1.4 Brown, Penelope & Levinson, Stephen C. (1987). *Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage*

**Citation Code**: {brown-levinson-1987-politeness}

**Key Findings**:
- **Face-Threatening Acts (FTAs)**: Speech acts that threaten hearer's "face" (public self-image)
  - **Positive face**: Desire to be approved/liked
  - **Negative face**: Desire not to be imposed upon
- **Commands** are inherently face-threatening (impose on negative face)
- **Politeness strategies to mitigate FTAs**:
  1. **Bald on-record**: Direct imperative ("Go!")
  2. **Positive politeness**: Emphasize solidarity ("Let's go together")
  3. **Negative politeness**: Minimize imposition ("Could you possibly...?")
  4. **Off-record**: Indirect hints ("It's cold in here" = request to close window)

- **Indirect speech acts** = primary politeness strategy:
  - Interrogative form, directive force: "Can you pass the salt?"
  - Reason: Preserves optionality (hearer can refuse without openly defying)

**Relevance for Our Algorithm**:
- **Target language requirements**:
  - **Japanese/Korean**: Imperative force requires **politeness level selection** (plain/polite/honorific)
  - **Javanese**: Register choice (ngoko/madya/krama) affects force interpretation
- **Biblical imperatives** vary in strength:
  - **Divine commands** (Gen 1:28 "Be fruitful"): Bald on-record (appropriate for God's authority)
  - **Jesus' teachings** (Matt 7:7 "Ask"): Moderate politeness (teacher-student relationship)
  - **Apostolic exhortations** (Eph 4:25): Mixed politeness (authoritative but fraternal)
- **Indirect requests**: How to annotate?
  - Example: "Will you not tell me?" (Gen 24:23) = interrogative form, directive force
  - Recommendation: Annotate primary force (Interrogative) + note indirect function

**Source**: [Brown and Levinson's Politeness Theory](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Brown-and-Levinsons-strategies-for-doing-an-FTA-1987-69_fig1_273945116)

### 1.5 Han, Chung-hye (2002). "Interpreting Interrogatives as Rhetorical Questions"

**Citation Code**: {han-2002-rhetorical}

**Key Findings**:
- **Rhetorical questions** have **opposite polarity illocutionary force**:
  - Positive RQ → Negative assertion: "Am I my brother's keeper?" = "I'm not..."
  - Negative RQ → Positive assertion: "Is anything too hard for the Lord?" = "Nothing is too hard"
- **Syntactic form**: Interrogative (question structure)
- **Pragmatic function**: Declarative (assertion, not information-seeking)
- **Identification criteria**:
  1. Speaker already knows answer
  2. No response expected
  3. Context makes answer obvious
  4. Often used for persuasion/emphasis

**Relevance for Our Algorithm**:
- **Genesis 4:9**: "Am I my brother's keeper?"
  - Cain's rhetorical question = evasion/denial
  - Mark as: **Interrogative** (form) + **Rhetorical flag** (function)
- **Romans 8:31**: "If God is for us, who can be against us?"
  - Negative RQ → Positive assertion: "No one can be against us"
- **Recommendation**: TBTA annotation policy:
  - **Illocutionary Force**: "Interrogative" (preserves syntactic form)
  - **Separate field**: "Rhetorical Question: True/False" (captures pragmatic reinterpretation)

**Source**: [Interpreting Interrogatives as Rhetorical Questions](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0024384101000444)

### 1.6 Sadock, Jerrold M. & Zwicky, Arnold M. (1985). "Speech Act Distinctions in Syntax"

**Citation Code**: {sadock-zwicky-1985-syntax}

**Key Findings**:
- **Sentence types** (syntactic categories) vs. **illocutionary force** (pragmatic categories):
  - Sentence types: Declarative, Interrogative, Imperative, Exclamative (syntactic)
  - Illocutionary force: Assertive, Directive, Commissive, etc. (pragmatic)
- **Many-to-many mapping**:
  - One sentence type → multiple forces (declarative syntax can be request: "I need help")
  - One force → multiple types (directive force via interrogative: "Can you...?")
- **Cross-linguistic variation**:
  - Some languages have dedicated exclamative syntax (Basque, Albanian)
  - Some collapse imperative/jussive (English uses "let" for both)
  - Some have more sentence types than English (Korean: promissive, propositive)

**Relevance for Our Algorithm**:
- **Annotation challenge**: Mark **form** or **function**?
  - TBTA choice: **Function** (pragmatic force)
  - Rationale: Translators need to know what the utterance **does**, not just how it's structured
- **Genesis 1:3**: "Let there be light"
  - English form: Quasi-imperative (periphrastic with "let")
  - Hebrew form: Jussive verb morphology (יְהִי)
  - Function: **Jussive** (3rd person directive)
  - TBTA annotation: "Jussive" (captures function, applicable across languages)

**Source**: Sadock & Zwicky in *Language Typology and Syntactic Description Vol. 1* (1985)

### 1.7 König, Ekkehard & Siemund, Peter (2007). "Speech Act Distinctions in Grammar"

**Citation Code**: {konig-siemund-2007-grammar}

**Key Findings**:
- **Typological survey** of how languages grammaticalize illocutionary force
- **Three levels of encoding**:
  1. **Lexical**: Performative verbs ("I promise", "I command")
  2. **Morphosyntactic**: Mood, word order, particles
  3. **Prosodic**: Intonation, stress

- **Imperative-Hortative-Jussive distinction**:
  - **Imperative**: 2nd person ("You go")
  - **Hortative**: 1st person plural inclusive ("Let's go")
  - **Jussive**: 3rd person or 1st person exclusive ("Let him/me go")
  - **Not universal**: Many languages collapse distinctions (English "let" for hortative/jussive)

- **Interrogative strategies** (aligned with WALS):
  - Particles most common (60%+ of languages)
  - Intonation alone less common than assumed
  - Verb morphology common in SOV languages

**Relevance for Our Algorithm**:
- **Biblical Hebrew** = Full three-way distinction (imperative/cohortative/jussive)
- **Target languages** may collapse:
  - English: "Let" for both hortative and jussive
  - Spanish: Subjunctive for both
- **Translation decision tree**:
  1. Identify Hebrew/Greek form (imperative/cohortative/jussive)
  2. Map to TBTA value (Imperative/Jussive)
  3. Determine target language encoding (subjunctive? particle? periphrastic?)
  4. Select appropriate form

**Source**: König & Siemund in *Language Typology and Syntactic Description* (2007)

### 1.8 Portner, Paul (2004). "The Semantics of Imperatives within a Theory of Clause Types"

**Citation Code**: {portner-2004-imperatives}

**Key Findings**:
- **Imperative semantics**: Not truth-conditional (can't be true/false)
- **To-Do List Semantics**: Imperatives add properties to hearer's "to-do list"
  - "Close the door" → Property: λx [x closes the door] added to hearer's to-do list
- **Illocutionary force ≠ Sentence type**:
  - Imperative sentence type has **default directive force**, but can have other forces:
    - Permission: "Go ahead and eat" (not command)
    - Wish/Blessing: "Have a nice day"
    - Conditional: "Touch it and you'll regret it"

**Relevance for Our Algorithm**:
- **Contextual force determination**:
  - Imperative morphology doesn't guarantee directive force
  - Genesis 2:16: "You may freely eat" (וְ אָכֹל תֹּאכַל) = permissive, not command
  - Must analyze **context + verb semantics** alongside morphology
- **Divine imperatives** vary:
  - Commands: "Do not eat" (Gen 2:17)
  - Blessings: "Be fruitful and multiply" (Gen 1:28) - command AND blessing?
  - Permissions: "From any tree you may eat" (Gen 2:16)

**Source**: Portner in *Semantics: An International Handbook* (2004)

### 1.9 Wierzbicka, Anna (2003). *Cross-Cultural Pragmatics*

**Citation Code**: {wierzbicka-2003-pragmatics}

**Key Findings**:
- **Cultural variation in directness**:
  - **English**: Preference for indirect requests ("Could you...?")
  - **Polish**: More direct ("Please give me...") = politeness, not rudeness
  - **Russian**: Imperatives common, mitigated by diminutives/particles
  - **Japanese**: Indirectness required for politeness (ne, deshou, etc.)

- **Command strategies differ**:
  - Anglo cultures: Minimize imposition ("If you could possibly...")
  - Slavic cultures: Directness + politeness markers ("Give me, please")
  - Asian cultures: Indirectness + honorifics

**Relevance for Our Algorithm**:
- **Target language selection**:
  - **English**: May use indirect interrogative for commands
  - **Japanese**: Requires imperative verb form + honorific suffix (-kudasai, -nasai)
  - **Russian**: Direct imperative acceptable with пожалуйста (pozhaluysta "please")
- **Biblical commands** need cultural adaptation:
  - Exodus 20:13 "You shall not murder" (לֹא תִרְצָח)
    - English: "shall not" (archaic/formal)
    - Japanese: 殺してはならない (koroshite wa naranai) = "must not kill" (prohibition form)
    - Spanish: No matarás (future tense with prohibitive force)

**Source**: Wierzbicka, *Cross-Cultural Pragmatics* (2003)

## 3. Biblical Language Sources

### 3.1 Biblical Hebrew Volitional Forms

**Citation Code**: {hebrew-volitional-forms}

**Sources**:
- Mounce, Bill. *Basics of Biblical Hebrew* (Chapter 18)
- unfoldingWord Hebrew Grammar: [Verb Jussive](https://uhg.readthedocs.io/en/latest/verb_jussive.html)

**Key Grammatical Points**:

#### Imperative
- **Person**: 2nd only (2ms, 2fs, 2mp, 2fp)
- **Formation**: Shortened form of imperfect, remove preformative
  - Root: הלך (halakh) "walk"
  - Imperfect 2ms: תֵּלֵךְ (telekh)
  - Imperative 2ms: לֵךְ (lekh) "Go!"
- **Function**: Direct command to addressee
- **Example**: Genesis 12:1 - לֶךְ-לְךָ (lekh-lekha) "Go forth"

#### Cohortative
- **Person**: 1st singular or plural
- **Formation**: Imperfect + ָה (-ah) suffix (often)
  - Imperfect 1cp: נֵלֵךְ (nelekh)
  - Cohortative 1cp: נֵלְכָה (nelkhah) "Let us go"
- **Function**: Self-encouragement, intention, hortative
- **Example**: Genesis 1:26 - נַעֲשֶׂה (na'aseh) "Let us make"
  - Note: This form identical to imperfect; context determines cohortative interpretation

#### Jussive
- **Person**: 3rd (all genders/numbers), sometimes 1st singular
- **Formation**: Shortened imperfect (apocopated)
  - Root: היה (hayah) "be"
  - Imperfect 3ms: יִהְיֶה (yihyeh)
  - Jussive 3ms: יְהִי (yehi) "Let there be"
- **Function**: Wish, command, or permission for 3rd party
- **Example**: Genesis 1:3 - יְהִי אוֹר (yehi 'or) "Let there be light"

**Negation**:
- Imperative/Cohortative/Jussive negated with אַל ('al), NOT לֹא (lo')
- אַל + jussive = prohibitive ("Do not...")

**Illocutionary Force Mapping**:
- Imperative → TBTA "Imperative"
- Cohortative → TBTA "Jussive" (hortative subtype)
- Jussive → TBTA "Jussive"

**Source**: [Hebrew Cohortative and Jussive](https://biblicalhebrew.org/understanding-the-cohortative-and-imperative-within-conditional-contexts.aspx)

### 3.2 Koine Greek Mood System

**Citation Code**: {greek-mood-system}

**Sources**:
- Mounce, Bill. *Basics of Biblical Greek* (Chapter 31)
- unfoldingWord Greek Grammar: [Mood](https://ugg.readthedocs.io/en/latest/mood.html)

**Four Moods**:

#### Indicative
- **Function**: Statements of reality/fact
- **Illocutionary Force**: Typically Declarative, but can be Interrogative with particles/intonation
- **Example**: John 11:35 - ἐδάκρυσεν (edakrysen) "Jesus wept"

#### Subjunctive
- **Function**: Uncertainty, purpose, hortative, deliberation
- **Illocutionary Force**: Variable (Jussive for hortative, Interrogative for deliberative)
- **Hortative Subjunctive**: 1st person plural exhortation
  - John 11:15 - ἄγωμεν (agōmen) "Let us go" → Jussive force
- **Deliberative Subjunctive**: Question about obligation
  - "What should we do?" → Interrogative force
- **Example**: Matthew 7:4 - ἄφες ἐκβάλω (aphes ekbalō) "Let me cast out" (subjunctive in purpose clause)

#### Optative
- **Function**: Wishes, polite requests, remote possibility
- **Illocutionary Force**: Jussive (wish) or Interrogative (polite question)
- **Rare in NT**: <70 occurrences
- **Example**: Romans 6:2 - μὴ γένοιτο (mē genoito) "May it never be!" → Jussive (strong negation)

#### Imperative
- **Function**: Commands, requests, exhortations
- **Illocutionary Force**: Imperative
- **Present Imperative**: Continuous action ("Keep praying")
- **Aorist Imperative**: Immediate/decisive action ("Pray now")
- **Example**: Matthew 3:2 - μετανοεῖτε (metanoeite) "Repent!" (present imperative)

**Illocutionary Force Mapping**:
- Indicative → Usually "Declarative" (sometimes "Interrogative")
- Subjunctive → "Jussive" (hortative) OR "Interrogative" (deliberative)
- Optative → "Jussive" (wish)
- Imperative → "Imperative"

**Source**: [Greek Mood System](https://ugg.readthedocs.io/en/latest/mood.html)

### 3.3 Pragmatics in Biblical Translation

**Citation Code**: {biblical-pragmatics}

**Source**: [Discourse Analysis & Pragmatics in Biblical Hebrew](https://biblicalhebrew.org/discourse-analysis-pragmatics-in-biblical-hebrew.aspx)

**Key Findings**:
- **Discourse analysis** tracks flow through:
  - Waw-consecutive (narrative chain)
  - Clause chaining
  - Lexical repetition
- **Pragmatics** uncovers:
  - Speaker intent
  - Social dynamics (honor/shame, power/solidarity)
  - Speech acts (commands, blessings, oaths, warnings)
  - Politeness strategies
  - Deixis (spatial/temporal reference)

**Illocutionary Force in Biblical Context**:
- **Divine commands** = maximum authority (no mitigation)
  - Genesis 1:3: "Let there be light" (divine fiat, performative)
  - Exodus 20:13: "You shall not murder" (apodictic law)
- **Human requests to God** = deference (mitigation expected)
  - Genesis 18:30: "Let not the Lord be angry" (Abraham's intercession - hedging)
- **Social hierarchy** affects force:
  - Superior → Inferior: Direct imperatives
  - Inferior → Superior: Indirect requests, petitions

**Translation Implication**:
- Target languages with honorifics (Japanese, Korean, Javanese) must encode **social register** alongside illocutionary force
- Divine speech requires **highest authority markers**
- Human-to-divine requires **highest deference markers**

### 4.1 Genesis 1:3 - "Let There Be Light"

**Citation Code**: {genesis-1-3-translation}

**Hebrew Analysis**:
- וַיֹּאמֶר אֱלֹהִים יְהִי אוֹר וַיְהִי-אוֹר
- vayomer Elohim yehi 'or vayehi-'or
- "And God said, 'Let there be light,' and there was light"
- **יְהִי (yehi)**: Qal imperfect 3ms jussive, apocopated ending of היה (hayah) "to be"

**Illocutionary Force**:
- **Form**: Jussive (3rd person volitional)
- **Function**: Divine fiat (performative declaration)
- **Theological Stake**: Creation ex nihilo—God's word creates reality

**Cross-Linguistic Translations**:

| Language | Translation | Strategy | Notes |
|----------|-------------|----------|-------|
| **English** | "Let there be light" | Periphrastic jussive (let + there be) | Preserves jussive force |
| **Spanish** | "Haya luz" | Subjunctive mood (haya = subjunctive of haber) | Grammatical jussive |
| **German** | "Es werde Licht" | Subjunctive I (werde) | Formal/liturgical |
| **French** | "Que la lumière soit" | Subjunctive with que | Que + subjunctive = jussive |
| **Latin** | "Fiat lux" | Jussive subjunctive (fiat) | Classical jussive |
| **Japanese** | 光あれ (hikari are) | Imperative of "be" (あれ are) | Literary/archaic, modern: 光よ、あれ |
| **Korean** | 빛이 있으라 (bichi issura) | Imperative form (-으라) | Formal command |
| **Mandarin** | 要有光 (yào yǒu guāng) | Auxiliary 要 (yào) "should/let" | Modal verb jussive |
| **Arabic** | ليكن نور (layakun nūr) | Jussive mood of كان (kāna) "to be" | Morphological jussive |
| **Swahili** | "Iwe nuru" | Subjunctive prefix i- | Bantu subjunctive |

**Key Insight**: Every language has a **jussive strategy**, but encoding varies widely:
- **Morphological** (Arabic, Latin): Jussive verb inflection
- **Syntactic** (English, French): Periphrastic construction
- **Modal** (Mandarin): Auxiliary verb
- **Mood** (Spanish, Swahili): Subjunctive

**Source**: [Genesis 1:3 Translation Analysis](https://hermeneutics.stackexchange.com/questions/54383/genesis-13-did-god-make-light)

### 4.2 Genesis 1:26 - "Let Us Make Man"

**Citation Code**: {genesis-1-26-translation}

**Hebrew Analysis**:
- וַיֹּאמֶר אֱלֹהִים נַעֲשֶׂה אָדָם בְּצַלְמֵנוּ כִּדְמוּתֵנוּ
- vayomer Elohim na'aseh 'adam bətsalmənu kidmutenu
- "And God said, 'Let us make man in our image, according to our likeness'"
- **נַעֲשֶׂה (na'aseh)**: Qal imperfect 1cp cohortative

**Grammatical Ambiguity**:
- Cohortative form **identical** to simple imperfect 1cp in this verb
- Context determines cohortative interpretation (command context, following jussives in Gen 1)

**Theological Significance**:
- **Trinitarian interpretation**: "Us" = Father, Son, Holy Spirit
- **Divine council interpretation**: "Us" = God + angelic council
- **Plural of majesty**: "We" as royal/majestic plural
- **Self-deliberation**: God speaking to himself

**Translation Challenges**:

1. **Clusivity**:
   - **Inclusive** (speaker + hearer): Implies angels included in creation (rejected by Christian orthodoxy)
   - **Exclusive** (speaker only, plural of majesty): Trinitarian persons (Christian orthodox view)
   - Languages with clusivity (Tagalog, Malay, Fijian):
     - **Tagalog**: Use **tayo** (inclusive) for Trinity persons, NOT **kami** (exclusive, excludes Son/Spirit)
     - **Malay**: Use **kita** (inclusive), NOT **kami** (exclusive)

2. **Number**:
   - **Dual** (exactly 2): HERETICAL—implies bitheism, excludes Holy Spirit
   - **Trial** (exactly 3): PREFERRED if available—encodes Trinity explicitly
   - **Plural** (3+): ACCEPTABLE—leaves number open
   - Languages with trial number (Hawaiian, Fijian): Use TRIAL form

3. **Illocutionary Force**:
   - **Cohortative/Hortative**: "Let us..." (1st person plural volitional)
   - **NOT Imperative**: God is not commanding humanity
   - **NOT Jussive** (3rd person): This is 1st person plural

**Cross-Linguistic Translations**:

| Language | Translation | Clusivity/Number | Notes |
|----------|-------------|------------------|-------|
| **English** | "Let us make" | N/A (no clusivity) | Periphrastic hortative |
| **Spanish** | "Hagamos" | Subjunctive 1cp | Hortative subjunctive |
| **Japanese** | 造ろう (tsukurou) | Volitional form 1cp | "Let's create" |
| **Korean** | 만들자 (mandeulja) | Hortative -자 | Plain hortative |
| **Tagalog** | **Tayo** ay lumikha | Inclusive pronoun | Includes Trinity persons |
| **Malay** | **Kita** mencipta | Inclusive pronoun | Includes Trinity persons |
| **Fijian** | (Data unavailable) | Use TRIAL if available | Exactly 3 persons |

### 4.3 Romans 8:31 - "Who Can Be Against Us?" (Rhetorical Question)

**Citation Code**: {romans-8-31-rhetorical}

**Greek Text**:
- εἰ ὁ θεὸς ὑπὲρ ἡμῶν, τίς καθ' ἡμῶν;
- ei ho theos hyper hēmōn, tis kath' hēmōn?
- "If God is for us, who against us?"

**Grammatical Form**: Interrogative (τίς = "who?")
**Illocutionary Force**: Declarative (rhetorical assertion)
**Pragmatic Meaning**: "No one can be against us" (negative assertion via positive rhetorical question)

**Rhetorical Question Markers**:
- Context (preceding affirmation of God's support)
- Unanswerable nature (obvious answer: "no one")
- Persuasive/emphatic function

**Translation Strategies**:

| Language | Translation | Strategy |
|----------|-------------|----------|
| **English** | "Who can be against us?" | Preserves interrogative form |
| **Spanish** | "¿Quién contra nosotros?" | Interrogative form |
| **Japanese** | 誰が私たちに敵対できようか (dare ga watashitachi ni tekitai dekiyou ka) | Interrogative + potential negative (できようか = "can do?") |
| **Korean** | 누가 우리를 대적하리요 (nuga urireul daejeokhariyo) | Interrogative + -리요 (rhetorical suffix) |
| **Mandarin** | 谁能抵挡我们呢 (shéi néng dǐdǎng wǒmen ne) | Interrogative + 呢 (ne, rhetorical particle) |

**Key Insight**: Some languages have **explicit rhetorical question markers**:
- **Korean**: -리요 (-riyo) suffix signals rhetorical question
- **Mandarin**: 呢 (ne) particle can mark rhetorical force
- **Japanese**: Potential negative form (dekiyou ka) implies impossibility

**TBTA Annotation Recommendation**:
- **Illocutionary Force**: "Interrogative" (preserves syntactic form)
- **Rhetorical Question**: True (separate boolean field)
- **Pragmatic Force**: Declarative (inferred from rhetorical flag)

## 5. Linguistic Theory: Form vs. Function

### 5.1 Mood vs. Illocutionary Force

**Citation Code**: {mood-vs-force}

**Source**: [Cambridge Handbook: Mood and Illocutionary Force](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/semantics/mood-and-illocutionary-force/DC8EC571ABB94D0C2FC37E5602995D20)

**Key Distinction**:
- **Mood**: **Morphological/grammatical** category (indicative, subjunctive, imperative, optative)
- **Illocutionary Force**: **Pragmatic/functional** category (assertive, directive, commissive, etc.)

**Relationship**:
- **Many-to-many mapping**:
  - One mood → multiple forces:
    - Greek subjunctive → Hortative (jussive force) OR Deliberative (interrogative force)
  - One force → multiple moods:
    - Directive force → Imperative mood OR Subjunctive mood OR Indicative (with intonation)

**Mood as IFID** (Illocutionary Force Indicating Device):
- Mood is **one signal** of illocutionary force, not a one-to-one mapping
- Other IFIDs: particles, word order, intonation, performative verbs, context

**Implication for TBTA**:
- TBTA annotates **illocutionary force** (pragmatic function), NOT grammatical mood
- Rationale: Translators need to know **what the utterance does**, which may differ from source language mood
  - Example: Greek optative (mood) → English subjunctive OR "may" + verb (no optative mood in English)

### 5.2 Performative Utterances

**Citation Code**: {performative-utterances}

**Source**: [Performative Utterance - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performative_utterance)

**Definition**:
- Utterances that **perform the act they name** rather than describe it
- **Explicit performatives**: Include performative verb ("I promise", "I pronounce", "I name")
- **Implicit performatives**: Lack explicit verb but still perform act ("Go!" = command, not description of commanding)

**Felicity Conditions** (Austin):
1. **Conventional procedure** must exist (e.g., marriage ceremony)
2. **Appropriate participants** (e.g., authorized officiant)
3. **Correct execution** (all steps followed)
4. **Sincerity** (speaker has relevant intentions/feelings)

**Biblical Performatives**:
1. **Divine Fiats** (Genesis 1):
   - "Let there be light" → Light exists (performative declaration)
   - Felicity conditions: God has authority to create by speaking

2. **Oaths and Covenants**:
   - Genesis 15:18 - "To your descendants I have given this land" (perfect tense = performative completion)

3. **Blessings and Curses**:
   - Numbers 6:24-26 - "The Lord bless you" (performative blessing)
   - Genesis 3:14 - "Cursed are you" (performative curse)

4. **Forgiveness**:
   - Matthew 9:2 - "Your sins are forgiven" (performative absolution)

**TBTA Annotation**:
- **Illocutionary Force**: "Declarative" (for explicit performatives with "I hereby...")
  - Rationale: Performatives have **declarative syntax**
- **Or "Jussive"**: For divine fiats (borderline between declaration and directive)
- **Separate tag?**: Consider "Performative: True/False" field to capture this special subtype

### 5.3 Indirect Speech Acts

**Citation Code**: {indirect-speech-acts}

**Source**: [What is an Indirect Speech Act?](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/pc.19009.mei)

**Definition**:
- Utterance with **primary illocutionary force** different from **literal/secondary force**
- Example: "Can you pass the salt?"
  - **Literal force**: Interrogative (question about ability)
  - **Primary force**: Directive (request to pass salt)

**Searle's Account**:
- Simultaneously realizes **two acts**:
  1. **Secondary act**: Literal meaning (interrogative)
  2. **Primary act**: Intended force (directive)
- Hearer infers primary force via **conversational implicature** (Grice)

**Motivations for Indirectness**:
1. **Politeness** (Brown & Levinson): Minimize face threat
2. **Deniability**: Speaker can back out ("I was just asking if you could")
3. **Convention**: Some indirect forms are conventionalized ("Could you...?" = standard request)

**Biblical Examples**:
1. **Genesis 3:9** - "Where are you?" (God to Adam)
   - **Literal**: Interrogative (question about location)
   - **Primary**: Could be Directive ("Come out") OR Declarative (rhetorical "I know where you are")

2. **John 21:5** - "Children, do you have any fish?"
   - **Literal**: Interrogative (yes/no question)
   - **Context suggests**: Directive ("Give me some fish") OR genuine question

**TBTA Annotation Challenge**:
- **Option 1**: Annotate **primary force** (what speaker intends)
  - Pro: Captures intended meaning
  - Con: Loses syntactic information
- **Option 2**: Annotate **literal force** + note indirectness
  - Pro: Preserves form-function mismatch
  - Con: Requires additional field
- **Recommendation**: Annotate **literal force** (Interrogative) + add "Indirect Speech Act: True/False" field
