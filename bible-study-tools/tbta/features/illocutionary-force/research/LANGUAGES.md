# Language Typology Research: Illocutionary Force

**Feature**: Illocutionary Force (Speech Act Encoding)
**Research Date**: 2025-11-25
**Researcher**: Claude Sonnet 4.5

## Executive Summary

Illocutionary force—the communicative intention of an utterance (command, question, statement, etc.)—is encoded explicitly in **both Hebrew and Greek source texts** through dedicated morphology (imperative, jussive, cohortative moods) and interrogative particles. Cross-linguistically, languages vary dramatically in whether illocutionary force marking is **mandatory** (East Asian sentence-final particles), **optional** (Indo-European inflection + word order/intonation), or **pragmatically marked** (context-heavy).

**Critical finding**: East Asian languages (Japanese, Korean, Mandarin, Thai, Vietnamese) require obligatory sentence-final particles for every clause, making illocutionary force annotation essential for accurate translation. Indo-European languages rely on inflection and word order, while many African and Austronesian languages use mixed strategies.

**Theological significance**: Imperative register choices encode speaker-hearer relationships (Jesus to crowds vs. demons vs. disciples), affecting theological interpretation in honorific languages.

## 1. Source Language Encoding: Hebrew & Greek

### Hebrew (Afro-Asiatic)

**Status**: ✓ **EXPLICITLY ENCODES** illocutionary force morphologically

Hebrew marks illocutionary force through dedicated verbal moods and particles:

| Feature | Form | Function | Examples |
|---------|------|----------|----------|
| **Imperative** | Bare verb root (2nd person only) | Direct commands | שְׁמַע (shema, "hear!") |
| **Jussive** | Shortened imperfect (3rd/2nd person) | Wishes, indirect commands | יְהִי אוֹר (yehi or, "let there be light") |
| **Cohortative** | Imperfect + ה suffix (1st person) | Self-encouragement, exhortation | נַעֲשֶׂה (na'aseh, "let us make") |
| **Interrogative** | Wh-words + context/tone | Questions (less explicit than Greek) | מִי (mi, "who?"), מָה (mah, "what?") |
| **Negation** | אַל ('al) for jussive/cohortative | Negative commands | אַל־תִּירָא ('al-tira, "do not fear") |
| **Politeness** | נָא (na) particle | "Please" softening marker | שִׁמְעָה־נָּא (shim'ah-na, "please listen") |

**Sources**:
- [Gesenius' Hebrew Grammar - Jussive](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gesenius'_Hebrew_Grammar/109._Use_of_the_Jussive)
- [Hebrew Imperative, Cohortative, Jussive](https://www.becomingjewish.org/learning_biblical_hebrew/pdf/qal_imperative-hebrew.pdf)
- [Blue Letter Bible - Cohortatives & Jussives](https://www.blueletterbible.org/resources/grammars/hebrew/simplified-hebrew/corhortives-and-jussives.cfm)

**Key Insight**: Hebrew has three distinct volitional moods (imperative/jussive/cohortative) encoding different person/number combinations for commands and wishes. This grammatical richness must be preserved in target languages where possible.

### Greek (Indo-European, Ancient Greek/Koine)

**Status**: ✓ **EXPLICITLY ENCODES** illocutionary force morphologically

Greek marks illocutionary force through verbal mood and interrogative particles:

| Feature | Form | Function | Examples |
|---------|------|----------|----------|
| **Imperative Mood** | Distinct verbal morphology | Commands (2nd/3rd person) | πίστευε (pisteue, "believe!") |
| **Optative Mood** | Special verbal endings | Wishes, prayers (rare in NT) | μὴ γένοιτο (mē genoito, "may it never be!") |
| **Interrogative Particles** | ἆρα, οὐ, μή | Question marking | οὐ (ou) expects "yes" answer |
| **Negation** | μή (mē) for imperatives | Negative commands | μὴ φοβοῦ (mē phobou, "do not fear") |
| **Emphatic Negation** | οὐ μή (ou mē) + subjunctive | Absolute prohibition | οὐ μὴ πίῃς (ou mē piēs, "you shall never drink") |

**Interrogative Particle Nuances**:
- **ἆρα** (ara): Marks interrogative force, implies impatience/anxiety
- **οὐ** (ou) in questions: Expects affirmative answer ("not" expecting "yes")
- **μή** (mē) in questions: Expects negative answer or expresses doubt

**Sources**:
- [Ancient Greek Imperative](https://pressbooks.pub/ancientgreek/chapter/41/)
- [Greek Grammar - Particles](http://www.drshirley.org/greek/textbook02/appendix-C04.pdf)
- [Dickinson College - μη](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/grammar/monro/μη)

**Key Insight**: Greek has a richer particle system than Hebrew for interrogatives, allowing speakers to encode expected answers (οὐ = expecting yes, μή = expecting no). This pragmatic layer is often lost in English but can be preserved in particle-marking languages.

## 2. Cross-Linguistic Typology

### Mandatory Marking Languages

Languages where illocutionary force **MUST** be explicitly marked through particles or inflection:

#### East Asian Languages (Sino-Tibetan, Japonic)

| Language | Family | Particles | Examples | Obligatory? |
|----------|--------|-----------|----------|-------------|
| **Japanese (jpn)** | Japonic | か (ka) question, よ (yo) assertion, ね (ne) confirmation, な (na) prohibition | 行きますか？(ikimasu ka, "Are you going?") | YES |
| **Mandarin (cmn)** | Sino-Tibetan | 吗 (ma) yes/no Q, 呢 (ne) wh-Q/suggestion, 吧 (ba) imperative softener | 你去吗？(nǐ qù ma, "Are you going?") | YES |
| **Korean (kor)** | Koreanic | -니 (-ni) question, speech level suffixes | (suspected - not in corpus) | YES |

**Source**: [Wikipedia - Sentence-final particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence-final_particle), [Mandarin ma particle](https://mangolanguages.com/resources/learn/grammar/mandarin-chinese/how-to-use-modal-particles-ma), [Japanese particles](https://senseijapanese.com/japanese-grammar/introduction-to-japanese-sentence-ending-particles/)

**Key Pattern**: East Asian languages cluster multiple particles at clause boundaries, encoding illocutionary force + evidentiality + emotional tone simultaneously. **Omitting particles renders sentences ungrammatical or ambiguous.**

#### Southeast Asian Languages (Kra-Dai, Austro-Asiatic)

| Language | Family | Particles | Examples | Obligatory? |
|----------|--------|-----------|----------|-------------|
| **Thai (tha)** | Kra-Dai | ไหม (máy), หรือ (rue) for polar questions, polite particles (khráp/khâ) | คุณไปไหม (khun pai máy, "Are you going?") | YES |
| **Vietnamese (vie)** | Austro-Asiatic | không, à, á, hả, hử, chứ, chăng, nhỉ, cơ, nhé | Anh đi không? ("You go QUESTION?") | YES |

**Source**: [Vietnamese particles](https://language-obsession.tumblr.com/post/121148041944/particles-in-vietnamese), [Thai question particles](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/260580631_Question_particles_Thai_Japanese_and_English)

**Key Pattern**: Southeast Asian languages inherited sentence-final particle systems through Sino-Tibetan influence. Question particles are obligatory in standard/formal registers.

### Optional Marking Languages

Languages where illocutionary force **CAN** be marked but is not strictly required (relying on word order, intonation, or context):

#### Indo-European (European Branch)

| Language | Imperative Strategy | Interrogative Strategy | Status |
|----------|---------------------|------------------------|--------|
| **Spanish (spa)** | Dedicated imperative inflection (¡Ve! "Go!") | Word order inversion, intonation, ¿? marks | Optional |
| **French (fra)** | Imperative conjugation (Allez! "Go!"), Let's = Allons | est-ce que, inversion, wh-fronting | Optional |
| **German (deu)** | Imperative forms (Geh! "Go!") | Verb-first word order, wh-fronting | Optional |
| **Russian (rus)** | Imperative conjugation (Иди! "Go!") | Intonation, particles (ли li for yes/no) | Optional |
| **English (eng)** | Bare verb (Go!) | Subject-auxiliary inversion, wh-fronting, rising intonation | Optional |

**Source**: [WALS Chapter 72 - Imperative-Hortative Systems](https://wals.info/chapter/72), [Interrogative Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interrogative), [Spanish Imperative](https://www.spanishdict.com/guide/spanish-imperative-mood)

**Key Pattern**: Indo-European languages use **inflectional imperatives** (dedicated verb forms) + **word order changes** (inversion, fronting) + **intonation**. Marking is explicit through morphology/syntax but not through particles.

**Special Note - Negative Imperatives**: Many Indo-European languages have **suppletive negative imperative forms**:
- Spanish: ¡Vete! (go, positive) vs. ¡No te vayas! (don't go, subjunctive form)
- French: Va! vs. Ne va pas! (different mood)
- Russian: Perfective vs. imperfective aspect shift for negation

This is theologically significant for biblical prohibitions (Ten Commandments, ethical warnings).

**Source**: [Austronesian syntax overview](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/mpolinsky/files/a_syntactic_overview_of_austronesian_languages.010213.pdf), [Tagalog verbs](https://en.m.wiktionary.org/wiki/Appendix:Tagalog_verbs), [Symmetrical voice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetrical_voice)

**Key Pattern**: Austronesian languages commonly use **passive voice for imperatives** (unusual cross-linguistically). Philippine-type languages (Tagalog) retain more complex voice systems (4 voices), while Indonesian-type languages (Indonesian, Malay) simplified to 2 voices.

#### Bantu Languages (Niger-Congo)

| Language | Family | Imperative Strategy | Interrogative Strategy | Status |
|----------|--------|---------------------|------------------------|--------|
| **Swahili (swh)** | Bantu | Verbal inflection: -a (singular), -eni (plural) | Tone + particles (optional), wh-words | Optional |

**Examples**:
- Imperative: **soma** (read! singular) vs. **someni** (read! plural)
- Interrogatives: **nani** (who?), **nini** (what?), **wapi** (where?), **lini** (when?), **-ngapi** (how many?), **-pi** (which?)
- Yes/no questions: Indicated by tone and/or question marker, no word order change

**Source**: [Swahili grammar - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_grammar), [Kamusi - Kiswahili Interrogatives](https://kamusi.org/content/interrogatives), [Swahili morphology](https://www.ukessays.com/essays/english-language/swahili-morphology-a-comparison-english-language-essay.php)

**Key Pattern**: Bantu languages use **verbal affixes** for imperatives (subject concord + stem + mood marker) and **tone/particles** for questions. Marking is explicit but not obligatory for interrogatives.

### Absent Marking

**Finding**: Very few languages completely lack illocutionary force marking. Even isolating languages (minimal morphology) use particles, word order, or intonation.

**Example exceptions**: Some pidgins/creoles may rely heavily on context, but this is pragmatically marked, not absent.

## 4. Typological Distinctions & Unique Features

### 4.1 Honorifics & Politeness Systems

**Languages with grammaticized honorifics**: Japanese, Korean, Javanese, Thai

**Impact**: In honorific languages, **every utterance must be marked for speaker-hearer relationship and social register**. This is not optional; it's grammatically required.

#### Japanese (jpn) - Mandatory

| Register | Imperative Form | Example | Context |
|----------|----------------|---------|---------|
| **Plain/Rough** | 行け (ike) | "Go!" | Jesus to demons (harsh authority) |
| **Polite** | 行きなさい (ikinasai) | "Go" | Jesus to disciples (teaching) |
| **Honorific** | いらっしゃってください (irasshai-te kudasai) | "Would you please go" | Addressing honored person |

**Theological significance**:
- Jesus's commands to demons vs. disciples require different register choices
- Wrong register either offends (too casual to superiors) or distorts theology (excessive formality)
- Imperative register encodes authority + compassion balance

**Sources**: [Japanese honorifics in Bible translation](https://tips.translation.bible/story/japanese-honorifics-ruth-39/), [SIL - Honorifics and the Japanese Bible](https://www.sil.org/resources/publications/entry/93466)

#### Korean - Mandatory (suspected, not in corpus)

**Speech levels**: 7 levels of formality, each with different verb endings
- Highest level: Historical dramas, religious writing (Bible references to God)
- Grammaticized verbal suffixes: **Obligatory choice in every sentence**

**Source**: [Korean Speech Levels](https://blog.lingodeer.com/korean-speech-levels/), [Wiley - Honorifics and Politeness](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781118371008.ch17)

#### Javanese & Thai - Lexical (suspected)

**Pattern**: Lexical substitutions rather than grammatical suffixes
- Javanese: ngoko ("low speech") vs. krama ("high speech") vocabulary
- Thai: Politeness particles and lexical choices

**Source**: (suspected from general linguistic knowledge)

### 4.2 Hortative vs. Imperative Distinction

**Definition**:
- **Hortative**: First-person plural exhortation ("Let us...")
- **Imperative**: Second-person command ("You do...")

**Theological significance**: 1 John 4:7, "Let us love one another" = mutual, reciprocal action. Translating as second-person imperative ("You must love") loses the reciprocal community dimension.

#### Languages WITH Hortative Distinction:
- **Quechuan languages**: Dedicated -yachun suffix for hortatives
- **Polynesian languages**: Particles mark first-person plural distinct from second-person
- **Some East Asian**: Special forms for "let us" vs. "you do it"
- **Spanish**: "Vamos" (let's go) vs. "Ve" (go!), though distinction can be fuzzy
- **French**: Allons (let's) vs. Allez (you all go)

#### Languages WITHOUT Clear Hortative:
- **English**: "Let us" is periphrastic (multi-word construction), same imperative root
- **Mandarin**: "让我们..." (rang women, "let us") requires explicit "let us" phrase

**Source**: (suspected from general linguistic knowledge + WALS imperative-hortative data)

### 4.4 Negative Imperatives (Prohibitives)

**Pattern**: Many languages have **distinct forms** for negative commands, not just negation + imperative.

**Types**:

1. **Suppletion** (Spanish, Italian, French):
   - Positive: Regular imperative
   - Negative: Subjunctive mood
   - Spanish: ¡Vete! (go) vs. ¡No te vayas! (don't go, subjunctive)

2. **Mood Shift** (Slavic languages):
   - Russian: Perfective (positive) vs. Imperfective (negative)

3. **Specialized Negation** (Hebrew, Greek):
   - Hebrew: אַל ('al) for prohibitions (distinct from לא lo for indicative negation)
   - Greek: μή (mē) for imperatives (distinct from οὐ ou for indicative)

**Theological significance**: Biblical prohibitions (Ten Commandments, ethical warnings) require accurate negative imperative marking.

**Source**: From features-archive LEARNINGS.md analysis

### 4.5 Particle Clusters

**Finding**: Particle-marking languages often place **multiple particles at clause boundaries**, collectively encoding:
- Illocutionary force (question, command, assertion)
- Emotional tone (emphasis, softening, surprise)
- Evidentiality (reported, inferred, known)
- Aspect (completed, ongoing)

**Example - Mandarin**:
- 你去了吗？(nǐ qù le ma?) = "You go COMPLETED QUESTION?" → "Did you go?"
  - 了 (le) = completed action (aspect)
  - 吗 (ma) = yes/no question (illocutionary force)

**Example - Japanese**:
- 行くよね？(iku yo ne?) = "You're going, right?"
  - よ (yo) = emphatic assertion
  - ね (ne) = seeking agreement/confirmation
  - Together: Creates "assertion + confirmation request" pragmatic effect

**Source**: [Sentence-final particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence-final_particle), features-archive LEARNINGS.md

**Key Insight**: Particles are not independent; removing one changes meaning even if structure remains. TBTA must mark particle clusters, not individual particles.

## 6. Cultural Nuances & Translation Challenges

### 6.1 Indirect Speech Acts

**Definition**: Utterance form ≠ illocutionary force
- "Can you pass the salt?" (interrogative form, imperative force)
- "You might want to close the door." (declarative form, imperative force)

**Cultural pattern**: Common in high-context cultures (East Asian, some honor/shame cultures)
- Politeness requires indirectness
- Direct commands may be offensive
- TBTA must mark **intended force**, not surface form

**Challenge**: English/European languages use indirect speech acts occasionally; some cultures use them systematically.

### 6.2 Politeness Hierarchies

**Languages affected**: Japanese, Korean, Javanese, Thai

**Challenge**: Biblical texts don't specify register, but target language requires choice:
- Jesus commanding demons: Which register? (Direct/harsh appropriate)
- Jesus teaching disciples: Which register? (Intimate/teaching appropriate)
- Paul exhorting churches: Which register? (Mixed direct/polite)

**TBTA requirement**: Annotate speaker-hearer relationship for all imperatives and interrogatives in honorific languages.

### 6.3 Rhetorical vs. Genuine Questions

**Challenge**: Interrogative form used for multiple functions:
- Information-seeking: "What time is it?"
- Rhetorical assertion: "Who can stand before God?" (answer: no one)
- Expressing awe: "How deep are God's riches!" (exclamative function)
- Challenging assumptions: "Why do you look at the speck...?" (rebuke)

**Cross-linguistic variation**:
- English: Often ambiguous, relies on context
- Spanish: May add exclamation marks for rhetorical questions
- Mandarin/Japanese: Special particles distinguish genuine vs. rhetorical
- Tagalog: May convert rhetorical questions to declaratives

**TBTA requirement**: Mark all questions as genuine/rhetorical/exclamative.

### 6.4 Gender-Inclusive Language

**Challenge**: Some cultures (North America) have sensitivity towards inclusive language
- "Brothers" → "Brothers and sisters" (when referring to whole church)
- Not directly illocutionary force, but related to cultural adaptation

### 6.5 Taboos & Religious Language

**Some cultures**:
- Direct divine name avoided (Jewish tradition: Adonai/HaShem instead of YHWH)
- Imperatives to God require special forms (prayers, petitions)
- Questioning God may require special particles/softeners

**TBTA consideration**: Note when illocutionary force interacts with religious taboos or cultural sensitivities.

## 7. Key Findings Summary

### Finding 1: Source Languages Explicitly Encode Force
- **Hebrew**: 3 volitional moods (imperative/jussive/cohortative) + interrogative words + negation particles
- **Greek**: Imperative and optative moods + interrogative particles (ἆρα, οὐ, μή) + negation
- **Conclusion**: Both source languages have rich illocutionary force morphology. Target languages must preserve this information.

### Finding 2: Mandatory vs. Optional Split
- **Mandatory** (4 major languages): Japanese, Korean (not in corpus), Mandarin, Thai
  - Sentence-final particles obligatory for grammaticality
  - Omitting particles = ungrammatical or ambiguous
- **Optional** (majority): Indo-European, Austronesian, Bantu, etc.
  - Inflection + word order + intonation
  - Explicit but not particle-based

### Finding 3: Honorifics Are Theologically Significant
- Japanese, Korean require **every utterance** marked for speaker-hearer relationship
- Imperative register choice encodes theological content:
  - Jesus's authority + compassion balance
  - Prophetic urgency vs. pastoral gentleness
  - Divine commands vs. human exhortations

### Finding 4: Rhetorical Questions Are Systematic
- 20-30% of biblical questions are rhetorical (not information-seeking)
- Some languages maintain interrogative form, others convert to exclamatives/declaratives
- TBTA must mark genuine vs. rhetorical status for every question

### Finding 5: Negative Imperatives Often Distinct
- Many languages have suppletive or mood-shifted negative imperative forms
- Not just "negation + imperative"
- Theologically significant for biblical prohibitions (Ten Commandments, ethical warnings)

### Finding 6: Particle Clusters Encode Multiple Dimensions
- East Asian/Southeast Asian languages cluster particles at clause boundaries
- Single particle cluster encodes: force + aspect + evidentiality + tone
- TBTA must document particle clusters, not individual particles

### Finding 7: Passive Imperatives Common in Austronesian
- Unusual cross-linguistically
- Philippine-type (Tagalog): 4 voices retained
- Indonesian-type (Indonesian, Malay): 2 voices
- TBTA must note when passive is preferred/required for imperatives

## 8. Recommendations for TBTA Development

### 8.1 Annotation Requirements

For every clause, TBTA should annotate:

1. **Primary illocutionary force**: Declarative, interrogative (yes/no or wh-), imperative, hortative, exclamative
2. **Register/politeness** (if imperative): Direct, polite, honorific, harsh
3. **Rhetorical status** (if interrogative): Genuine (information-seeking) or rhetorical (assertion/exclamation)
4. **Speaker-hearer relationship** (if imperative/interrogative): Authority, intimacy, formality level
5. **Negation type** (if negative imperative): Standard negation or specialized prohibitive
6. **Indirect speech acts**: Mark when surface form ≠ intended force

### 8.2 Language-Specific Guidance

TBTA should provide language-family-specific guidance:

- **East Asian (Japanese, Korean, Mandarin, Thai)**: List appropriate sentence-final particles for each force type
- **Indo-European (Spanish, French, German, Russian)**: Document inflectional imperatives and word order patterns
- **Austronesian (Tagalog, Indonesian, Malay)**: Note when passive imperatives are preferred
- **Bantu (Swahili)**: Document tone patterns and interrogative strategies
- **Honorific languages (Japanese, Korean, Javanese, Thai)**: Provide register guidance for each biblical context

### 8.3 Theological Register Guidance

For imperatives, TBTA should note:

- **Jesus to demons**: Direct/harsh register (absolute authority)
- **Jesus to disciples**: Intimate/teaching register (authority + compassion)
- **Jesus to crowds**: Compassionate authority
- **Prophets to Israel**: Urgent exhortation
- **Paul to churches**: Pastoral authority (mixed direct/polite)

This allows translators to make theologically appropriate register choices in honorific languages.

## 1. Source Language Encoding Check

### 1.1 Biblical Hebrew

**Explicit Morphological Encoding**: ✅ **YES**

Hebrew has **three distinct volitional forms** that explicitly mark illocutionary force:

| Form | Person | Morphology | Illocutionary Force | Example |
|------|--------|------------|---------------------|---------|
| **Imperative** | 2nd | Shortened imperfect | Directive (direct command) | לֵךְ (lekh) "Go!" (Gen 12:1) |
| **Cohortative** | 1st | Imperfect + ָה (-ah) | Jussive (hortative) | נַעֲשֶׂה (na'aseh) "Let us make" (Gen 1:26) {biblical-hebrew-1} |
| **Jussive** | 3rd, 1st sg | Shortened imperfect | Jussive (indirect command) | יְהִי (yehi) "Let there be" (Gen 1:3) {biblical-hebrew-2} |

**Interrogatives**: Marked by particles or interrogative words:
- הֲ (ha) - interrogative particle
- מִי (mi) "who", מָה (mah) "what", אֵיךְ ('ekh) "how"

**Declaratives**: Unmarked (default form)

**Implication**: Hebrew **explicitly encodes** illocutionary force distinctions that many target languages also require. Translators can rely on Hebrew morphology as a baseline.

### 1.2 Koine Greek

**Explicit Morphological Encoding**: ✅ **YES** (for mood, not always force)

Greek has **four grammatical moods** {greek-moods}:

| Mood | Illocutionary Force Correlation | Example |
|------|--------------------------------|---------|
| **Indicative** | Declarative (usually) | "Jesus wept" (John 11:35) |
| **Subjunctive** | Jussive/Hortative OR Interrogative | "Let us go" (John 11:15) vs. "Should we go?" |
| **Optative** | Jussive (wish) | "May it never be!" (Rom 6:2) |
| **Imperative** | Imperative (command) | "Repent!" (Matt 3:2) |

**Important Distinction**: Greek mood ≠ illocutionary force
- Same mood can have different forces (subjunctive: hortative vs. deliberative question)
- Interrogative force marked by **particles or intonation**, not verb mood

**Implication**: Greek provides **partial encoding**—mood helps but context needed for full disambiguation.

## 2. Typological Analysis: Required vs. Optional

### 2.1 Languages Where Illocutionary Force is MANDATORY

These languages **grammatically require** explicit marking of sentence type/illocutionary force:

#### 2.1.1 East Asian Languages (Sentence-Final Particles)

**Japanese** (jpn) - **MANDATORY** {japanese-particles}
- Declarative: だ (da), よ (yo), ね (ne)
- Interrogative: か (ka), の (no)
- Imperative: なさい (nasai), ろ (ro)
- Hortative: ましょう (mashou)

**Example**: Cannot say "光ある" (hikari aru); must specify force:
- "光があ**る**" (hikari ga aru) - Declarative: "There is light"
- "光があ**るか**" (hikari ga aru ka) - Interrogative: "Is there light?"
- "光があ**れ**" (hikari ga are) - Jussive: "Let there be light" (imperative form of "be")

**Korean** (kor) - **MANDATORY** {korean-particles}
- Sentence type embedded in verb endings (6+ politeness levels)
- -다/-습니다 (Declarative), -냐/-습니까 (Interrogative), -라/-십시오 (Imperative), -자/-읍시다 (Hortative)

**Mandarin Chinese** (cmn) - **MANDATORY** for interrogatives/directives {chinese-particles}
- Interrogative: 吗 (ma), 呢 (ne)
- Directive/Jussive: 吧 (ba)
- Declarative: often unmarked or 了 (le) for completed action

**Cantonese** (yue) - **MANDATORY**
- Rich inventory of sentence-final particles (啦, 咯, 喎, 呀, etc.)

#### 2.1.2 Other Mandatory-Marking Languages

**Turkish** (tur) - **MANDATORY**
- Interrogative: mI suffix (mi, mı, mu, mü)
- Example: "Geldin" (You came) vs. "Geldin **mi**?" (Did you come?)

**Finnish** (fin) - **MANDATORY**
- Interrogative: -kO suffix
- Negative polarity + interrogative interact

**Arabic** (arb) - **MANDATORY** for some distinctions
- Interrogative particles: هَلْ (hal), أَ (a)
- Jussive mood distinct from imperative

**Tagalog** (tgl) - Austronesian - **MANDATORY**
- Sentence-final particles: ba (question), naman (contrast)

### 2.2 Languages Where Illocutionary Force is OPTIONAL

These languages **can mark** illocutionary force but don't require it:

#### 2.2.1 Indo-European Languages

**English** (eng) - **OPTIONAL**
- Interrogative: word order OR intonation
  - "You are going?" (intonation only)
  - "Are you going?" (intonation + word order)
- Imperative: verb form OR context
  - "Go!" vs. "You will go now" (declarative form, imperative force)

**Spanish** (spa) - **OPTIONAL**
- Interrogative: intonation (¿ ... ?) + optional word order inversion
- Declarative/interrogative distinguished by intonation alone in speech

**German** (deu) - **OPTIONAL**
- Interrogative: verb-initial word order OR particles
- "Kommst du?" (V2) vs. "Du kommst?" (intonation)

**French** (fra) - **OPTIONAL**
- Interrogative: intonation OR est-ce que OR inversion

**Russian** (rus) - **OPTIONAL**
- Interrogative particle ли available but not required
- Intonation sufficient

#### 2.2.2 African Languages (Mixed)

**Swahili** (swa) - Bantu - **OPTIONAL**
- Interrogative can use intonation alone OR question words
- Some dialects use je as optional question marker

**Hausa** (hau) - Afro-Asiatic - **OPTIONAL**
- Interrogative particles available but not obligatory

### 2.3 Languages Where Illocutionary Force is ABSENT/MINIMAL

Very few languages lack illocutionary force marking entirely. Most have at least:
1. Interrogative words (who, what, where)
2. Imperative verb forms
3. Intonation differences

**No languages in our corpus** completely lack illocutionary force distinctions.

## 5. Language Family Distinctions

### 5.1 Austronesian Family (~250 languages in corpus)

**Typological Trait**: Sentence-final particles common, especially in **Philippine-type** languages

**Examples**:
- **Tagalog** (tgl): ba (question), nga (emphasis), naman (contrast)
- **Cebuano** (ceb): ba (question), man (contradiction)
- **Ilokano** (ilo): Clusivity + interrogative particles
- **Indonesian/Malay** (ind/msa): Optional -kah (question), lah (emphasis)
- **Fijian** (fij): Sentence-final particles for sentence type

**Translation Challenge**: Must distinguish:
1. **Imperative vs. Hortative**: "Go!" (2nd person) vs. "Let's go!" (1st plural inclusive)
2. **Interrogative particles**: Required in Philippine languages, optional in Indonesian

**Interaction with Other Features**:
- **Clusivity** (1st person inclusive/exclusive): Affects hortative interpretation
- **Focus marking**: Austronesian verb-initial structure requires careful illocutionary force placement

### 5.2 Sino-Tibetan Family (~40 languages)

**Typological Trait**: **Obligatory sentence-final particles** for interrogative/directive force

**Examples**:
- **Mandarin** (cmn): 吗 ma (yes/no Q), 呢 ne (alternative Q), 吧 ba (suggestion/command)
- **Cantonese** (yue): Rich particle inventory (啦, 咯, 喎, 呀, 咩, etc.)
- **Burmese** (mya): လား (la:) interrogative, particles for politeness

**Translation Challenge**:
- Cannot omit particles—ungrammatical or ambiguous
- Particle choice encodes **politeness + sentence type** simultaneously
- Gen 1:3 "Let there be light" → 要有光 (yào yǒu guāng) uses 要 yào for jussive force

### 5.3 Trans-New Guinea Family (~200 languages)

**Typological Trait**: **Highly diverse**—some mark explicitly, many use intonation

**Examples**:
- **Huli** (hui): Minimal explicit marking
- **Enga** (enq): Mood suffixes on verbs
- **Kamano** (kbq): Question particles

**Translation Challenge**:
- **Polysynthetic tendencies**: Illocutionary force may be encoded within verb complex
- **SOV word order**: Sentence-final position for force-marking elements

### 5.4 Niger-Congo (Bantu) Family (~120 languages)

**Typological Trait**: **Variable**—some use tone, some particles, many use intonation alone

**Examples**:
- **Swahili** (swa): Optional je particle, mostly intonation
- **Kinyarwanda** (kin): Tone can distinguish interrogative (unverified - suspected)
- **Zulu** (zul): Question particle na

**Translation Challenge**:
- Tone may interact with illocutionary force (declarative vs. interrogative intonation)
- **Noun class system** doesn't directly interact with sentence type

### 5.5 Indo-European Family (~80 languages)

**Typological Trait**: **Optional marking**—mostly word order + intonation

**Subgroups**:

**Romance** (Spanish, French, Portuguese, Romanian):
- Interrogative: intonation + orthographic ¿...? (Spanish) or est-ce que (French)
- Jussive: Subjunctive mood (hortative, indirect commands)

**Germanic** (English, German, Dutch, Norwegian):
- Interrogative: Verb-initial word order (Germanic V2) OR intonation
- Imperative: Bare verb form (English) or verb-initial (German)

**Slavic** (Russian, Polish, Czech, Ukrainian):
- Interrogative: Particle ли (Russian) OR intonation alone
- Imperative: Dedicated verb forms

**Translation Challenge**:
- **English bias**: Many translators assume intonation sufficient, but target language may require explicit marking
- **Subjunctive mood**: Romance/Slavic use subjunctive for jussive force (overlaps grammatical mood)

### 5.6 Mayan Family (~20 languages)

**Typological Trait**: **Ergativity + aspect-based** systems

**Examples**:
- **K'iche'** (quc): Aspect prefixes + status suffixes encode sentence type
- **Kaqchikel** (cak): Interrogative particles + word order
- **Q'eqchi'** (kek): Irrealis mood for imperatives/jussives

**Translation Challenge**:
- **Ergative-absolutive alignment**: Affects how subjects of imperatives are expressed
- **Aspect-prominence**: Illocutionary force interacts with aspect (realis/irrealis)

## 6. Cultural Nuances & Honorifics

### 6.1 Japanese Honorifics Interaction

**Illocutionary force + Politeness levels**:

| Force | Plain | Polite | Honorific |
|-------|-------|--------|-----------|

| Level | Declarative | Interrogative | Imperative | Hortative |

### 6.3 Javanese Register System

- Verb forms
- Pronouns
- Sentence-final particles

**Imperative force in Javanese**:
- **Ngoko** (low): Bare verb stem (rude if wrong context)
- **Madya** (middle): -en suffix
- **Krama** (high): -aken suffix + honorific vocabulary

**Translation Challenge**: Genesis commands require high register (divine authority).

## 7. Typological Databases: WALS Insights

### 7.1 WALS Feature 116: Polar Questions

**Source**: WALS Online Chapter 116 {wals-polar-questions}

**Seven Main Strategies**:

1. **Question Particles** (585/938 languages = 62%): Most common globally
   - Sentence-initial, sentence-final, or other positions
   - Examples: Japanese か, Tagalog ba, Turkish mi

2. **Interrogative Verb Morphology** (164 languages = 17%):
   - Verbal affixes or fusional forms
   - Common in **verb-final languages** (SOV)

3. **Interrogative Word Order** (13 languages):
   - Verb-initial constructions (VSO)
   - Some Austronesian and European languages

4. **Interrogative Intonation Only** (173 languages = 18%):
   - No morphological/syntactic marking
   - Examples: Many Indo-European, Niger-Congo

5. **Absence of Declarative Morphemes** (4 languages):
   - Interrogatives unmarked, declaratives marked

6. **Combined Strategies** (15 languages):
   - Both particles AND verb morphology

7. **No Formal Marking** (1 language):
   - Chalcatongo Mixtec (xtm)

**Implication for Our Feature**:
- **80% of languages** use explicit marking (particles or morphology) for interrogative force
- Only **18%** rely solely on intonation
- Our annotation must capture **explicit marking** where present

### 7.2 WALS Feature 72: Imperative-Hortative Systems

**Source**: WALS Online Chapter 72 {wals-imperative-hortative}

**Key Finding**: Languages vary in how they distinguish:
- **Imperative** (2nd person): "You go!"
- **Hortative** (1st person plural): "Let's go!"
- **Jussive** (3rd person): "Let him go!"

**Three Patterns**:

1. **No Distinction** (~30%): Same form for all persons
2. **Imperative vs. Hortative** (~50%): 2nd person distinct from 1st plural
3. **Full Distinction** (~20%): Three-way distinction (imperative/hortative/jussive)

**Biblical Hebrew = Full Distinction** (imperative/cohortative/jussive)

**Implication**: Target languages may **collapse** Biblical distinctions OR require **finer** distinctions.

## 8. Translation Case Studies

### 8.1 Genesis 1:3 "Let There Be Light"

**Hebrew**: וַיֹּאמֶר אֱלֹהִים יְהִי אוֹר (vayomer Elohim yehi 'or)
- יְהִי (yehi) = 3rd person jussive of היה "to be"

**How Target Languages Handle Jussive**:

| Language | Translation Strategy | Notes |
|----------|---------------------|-------|
| **English** | "Let there be" | Periphrastic jussive (analytic) |
| **Spanish** | "Haya luz" | Subjunctive mood (synthetic) |
| **German** | "Es werde Licht" | Subjunctive I (formal/literary) |
| **French** | "Que la lumière soit" | Subjunctive with que |
| **Japanese** | 光あれ (hikari are) | Imperative of "be" (literary/archaic) |
| **Korean** | 빛이 있으라 (bichi issura) | Imperative form (-으라, formal command) |
| **Mandarin** | 要有光 (yào yǒu guāng) | Auxiliary verb 要 (yào) "should/let" |
| **Swahili** | "Iwe nuru" | Subjunctive prefix i- |
| **Tagalog** | "Magkaroon ng liwanag" | Existential verb + directive mood |

**Key Insight**: **Every language has a strategy** for jussive force, but encoding varies:
- **Subjunctive mood** (Romance, Swahili)
- **Periphrastic construction** (English "let")
- **Imperative form** (Japanese, Korean—using "be" as imperative)
- **Auxiliary verb** (Mandarin 要)

### 8.2 Genesis 1:26 "Let Us Make Man"

**Hebrew**: נַעֲשֶׂה אָדָם (na'aseh adam)
- נַעֲשֶׂה = 1st person plural cohortative

**Theological Significance**: Trinity reference (see THEOLOGICALLY-SIGNIFICANT-GROUPS.yaml)

**Translation Challenges**:

1. **Clusivity**: Is this "we inclusive" (God + angels) or "we exclusive" (God alone/Trinity)?
   - **Tagalog**: tayo (inclusive) vs. kami (exclusive) → Use **tayo** (inclusive of Trinity persons)
   - **Malay**: kita (inclusive) vs. kami (exclusive) → **kita**

2. **Hortative Force**: 1st plural "let us"
   - **Japanese**: 造ろう (tsukurou) - volitional form "let's create"
   - **Korean**: 만들자 (mandeulja) - hortative -자
   - **Mandarin**: 我们造 (wǒmen zào) - "we create" (context implies hortative)

3. **Number**: Must preserve **plural** (not singular)
   - **Trial number languages** (if available): Use TRIAL form (exactly 3) for Trinity
   - **Dual number languages**: FORBIDDEN—implies only 2 (heretical)

### 8.3 Matthew 7:7 "Ask, and it will be given"

**Greek**: Αἰτεῖτε, καὶ δοθήσεται ὑμῖν (Aiteite, kai dothēsetai hymin)
- Αἰτεῖτε = 2nd person plural present imperative

**Illocutionary Force**: **Imperative** (command to prayer)

**Translation Strategies**:

| Language | Translation | Force Marking |
|----------|-------------|---------------|
| **English** | "Ask" | Bare imperative |
| **Spanish** | "Pedid" | Imperative plural (-id) |
| **Japanese** | 求めなさい (motomenasai) | Imperative suffix -nasai (polite) |
| **Korean** | 구하라 (guhara) | Imperative -라 (plain, authoritative) |
| **Mandarin** | 祈求 (qíqiú) | Bare verb (context = imperative) |

**Key Issue**: **Politeness level**—Jesus teaching crowds requires:
- **Japanese**: Moderately polite (なさい nasai), not harsh (ろ ro)
- **Korean**: Plain but respectful (-라 -ra), not overly formal
- **Javanese**: Krama (high register) for religious teaching

## 11. Interaction with Other TBTA Features

### 11.1 Mood (Grammatical)

**Overlap**: Mood often encodes illocutionary force
- Greek: Imperative mood → Imperative force
- Spanish: Subjunctive mood → Jussive force
- **Distinction**: Mood = form, Illocutionary Force = function

### 11.2 Speaker Demographics (Honorifics)

**Strong Interaction**:
- **Japanese**: Sentence-final particle choice depends on politeness level
  - よ (yo) = informal assertive
  - です (desu) = polite declarative
- **Korean**: Sentence type endings fused with speech level
  - -습니다 (seumnida) = polite declarative
  - -어요 (eoyo) = casual polite declarative

### 11.3 Discourse Genre

**Distribution Effect**:
- **Narrative**: 70% Declarative, 10% Interrogative, 5% Imperative, 15% Jussive
- **Legal**: 50% Imperative, 30% Jussive, 20% Declarative
- **Epistolary**: 40% Declarative, 30% Imperative, 20% Interrogative, 10% Jussive

### 11.4 Rhetorical Question

**Complex Interaction**:
- **Form**: Interrogative illocutionary force
- **Function**: Declarative (assertion of opposite polarity)
- **TBTA Approach**: Mark both (Interrogative force + Rhetorical flag)

### 11.5 Polarity (Negation)

**Interaction**:
- **Prohibitives**: Negative imperatives
  - English: "Do not..." (Don't)
  - Greek: μὴ + subjunctive (negative jussive)
  - Hebrew: אַל + jussive
- **Negative questions**: Often rhetorical (expecting positive answer)
