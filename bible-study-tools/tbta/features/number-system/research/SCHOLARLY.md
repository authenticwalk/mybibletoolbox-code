# Scholarly Research: Number System

**Feature**: Grammatical Number
**Scope**: Linguistic typology, Bible translation theory, cross-linguistic variation
**Last Updated**: 2025-11-26

## Executive Summary

Grammatical number systems range from simple two-way (singular/plural) to complex five-way distinctions (singular/dual/trial/paucal/plural). **Corbett (2000)** provides the foundational typological survey, documenting ~88 languages with dual, ~20-30 with trial (mostly Austronesian/Oceanic), and ~30-50 with paucal. **Greenberg's Universal 34** establishes the implicational hierarchy: no trial without dual, no dual without plural. Biblical Hebrew marks dual morphologically (Gesenius §88), while Koine Greek lost the dual by the Hellenistic period. **Theological challenge**: Genesis 1:26 "Let us" translations vary by target language number system—trial languages (e.g., Larike) can explicitly encode Trinity as "exactly 3 persons," while dual languages (e.g., Hawaiian) risk Binitarian heresy if dual is used. Bible translation theory (Nida 1964, Beekman & Callow 1974) addresses obligatory categories in receptor languages that do not exist in source languages.

**Key Findings**:
- **172 trial language claim** (TBTA Edge Cases) vs. **~30 confirmed** (Corbett 2000) - requires verification
- **Trial restricted to pronouns** in all documented cases—no language marks trial on nouns
- **Animacy hierarchy** governs obligatoriness (mandatory for humans, optional for inanimates)
- **Associative plural** ("X and company") documented in Austronesian but not addressed in TBTA

## 1. Scholarly Sources

### 1.1 Corbett, Greville G. (2000). *Number*. Cambridge University Press.

**Citation Code**: {corbett-2000}
**URL**: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/number/497D34AB7181174CB329E8358EB2BC36

**Key Findings**:
- **Comprehensive typological survey**: Documents number systems across ~200 languages
- **Determinate vs. Indeterminate**: Singular/Dual/Trial are exact counts; Paucal/Plural are inexact
- **Implicational hierarchy**: Trial implies Dual implies Plural
- **Quadrial**: No natural language has true grammatical quadrial (contrary to TBTA schema)
- **Trial languages**: ~20-30 confirmed (mostly Austronesian/Oceanic: Larike, Tok Pisin, Marshallese, Lihir, Tolomako, Manam)
- **Paucal languages**: ~30-50 (Arabic 3-10, Warndarrang ~5, Baiso 2-6, Murrinh-patha ~10-15)
- **Dual languages**: ~88 documented

**Quote** (p. 39):
> "Corbett makes a distinction between determinate and indeterminate numbers. Singular, dual and trial denoting exactly one, two or three individuals, respectively, are determinate numbers; paucal and plural are indeterminate ones."

**Relevance**: Foundational work for understanding TBTA's number values. Refutes Quadrial, confirms Trial rarity.

### 1.2 Greenberg, Joseph H. (1963). "Some Universals of Grammar with Particular Reference to the Order of Meaningful Elements." *Universals of Language*.

**Citation Code**: {greenberg-1963}
**URL**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_number (referenced)

**Key Findings**:
- **Universal 34**: "No language has a trial number unless it has a dual. No language has a dual unless it has a plural."
- **Implicational Hierarchy**: Singular < Plural < Dual < Trial
- **Predictive power**: If a language has Trial, it must have Dual and Plural (verified in Larike, Marshallese, Tok Pisin)

**Relevance**: Establishes constraints on possible number systems. Explains why Quadrial is not attested.

### 1.3 Gesenius, Wilhelm. (1910). *Gesenius' Hebrew Grammar*. Edited by E. Kautzsch. Oxford University Press.

**Citation Code**: {gesenius-1910}
**URL**: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gesenius'_Hebrew_Grammar/88._Of_the_Dual

**Key Findings** (§88 - "Of the Dual"):
- **Dual formation**: Suffix -ַיִם (-ayim) for masculine, -ָתַיִם (-tayim) for feminine
- **Usage**: "Confined...to those objects which are by nature or art always found in pairs"
- **Body parts**: יָדַיִם (yadayim "two hands"), עֵינַיִם (enayim "two eyes"), רַגְלַיִם (raglayim "two feet")
- **Time expressions**: יוֹמַיִם (yomayim "two days"), שְׁנָתַיִם (shenatayim "two years")
- **Frozen duals**: שָׁמַיִם (shamayim "heavens"), מַיִם (mayim "waters"), מִצְרַיִם (mitsrayim "Egypt")
- **Agreement**: "Subjects in the dual are construed with the plural of the predicate, since verbs, adjectives, and pronouns...have no dual forms"

**Quote**:
> "The use of the dual in Hebrew is confined, except in the numerals 2, 12, 200, &c., practically to those objects which are by nature or art always found in pairs, especially to the double members of the body."

**Relevance**: Authoritative source for Hebrew dual morphology. Explains semantic vs. morphological tension in TBTA (frozen duals like shamayim).

### 1.4 Wallace, Daniel B. (1996). *Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics: An Exegetical Syntax of the New Testament*. Zondervan.

**Citation Code**: {wallace-1996}
**URL**: https://archive.org/details/greekgrammarbeyo0000wall

**Key Findings**:
- **Loss of dual in Koine**: "With regard to number (i.e., singular or plural), the Greek will always use one or the other. There was a dual number in Attic Greek, but it had disappeared by the time of NT Koine."
- **Singular/Plural only**: NT Greek cannot distinguish dual (2) vs. trial (3) vs. paucal (few) vs. plural (many)
- **Morphology**: Plural forms substitute for all 2+ entities
- **Implication**: Target languages with finer number distinctions must infer from context (e.g., "two disciples" Luke 24:13)

**Relevance**: Establishes source language limitation. Greek translations into dual/trial languages require contextual analysis.

### 1.5 Nida, Eugene A. (1964). *Toward a Science of Translating*. Brill.

**Citation Code**: {nida-1964}
**URL**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Nida

**Key Findings**:
- **Formal Equivalence (F-E)**: Reproduces form and content of original (word-for-word)
- **Dynamic Equivalence (D-E)**: Equivalence of **response** rather than form (thought-for-thought)
- **Obligatory categories**: Target languages may require grammatical categories absent in source (e.g., dual/trial)
- **No absolute correspondence**: "No two languages are identical...hence, there can be no fully exact translations"

**Quote**:
> "A translation of dynamic equivalence aims at complete naturalness of expression, and tries to relate the receptor to modes of behavior relevant within the context of his own culture."

**Relevance**: Theoretical framework for handling number mismatches. If Hebrew lacks trial, but target language requires it, translator must infer semantic intent.

### 1.6 Beekman, John & Callow, John. (1974). *Translating the Word of God*. Zondervan.

**Citation Code**: {beekman-callow-1974}
**URL**: https://archive.org/details/translatingwordo0000beek

**Key Findings**:
- **Semantic vs. grammatical classes**: "Matching semantic and grammatical classes" in translation
- **Obligatory categories**: "Difficulties...from obligatory categories in receptor languages which do not exist in the biblical languages"
- **Idiomatic approach**: Recommends dynamic equivalence for naturalness

**Relevance**: SIL/Wycliffe standard for Bible translation. Addresses problem of number mismatch (e.g., Hebrew dual → target language without dual).

### 1.7 Biblical Hebrew Dual Morphology. (n.d.). *BiblicalHebrew.org*.

**Citation Code**: {biblicalhebrew-dual}
**URL**: https://biblicalhebrew.org/dual-form-and-its-limited-use-in-hebrew.aspx

**Key Findings**:
- **Dual suffix**: -ַיִם (-ayim) in absolute, -ֵי (-ê) in construct
- **Natural pairs**: Body parts (eyes, hands, feet), time expressions (two days)
- **Lexicalized duals**: "Not all nouns with a dual suffix semantically refer to a pair. For instance, שָׁמַיִם ('heavens') and מַיִם ('water') appear morphologically dual but function grammatically as plurals."
- **Agreement**: "Verbs agree with dual subjects using the plural forms" (no dual verb conjugation)

**Quote**:
> "Though these appear dual morphologically, they function syntactically as plural nouns. Scholars often call them frozen duals—forms that retain dual morphology but plural function."

**Relevance**: Explains TBTA's semantic priority for frozen duals (mark as Singular despite dual morphology).

### 1.8 Moulton, J. H. (1908). "The Dual in Koine Greek." *A Grammar of New Testament Greek*.

**Citation Code**: {moulton-1908-dual}
**URL**: https://koine-greek.com/2008/12/15/moulton-on-the-greek-dual/

**Key Findings**:
- **Dual loss**: "In the Classical period, the dual was viable only in Attic. In the Koine and in New Testament Greek, the dual has virtually disappeared."
- **Plural substitution**: "The dual number, which denoted pairs in Classical Greek, was entirely lost in Koine, with plural forms substituting for it"
- **No NT occurrences**: Zero instances of dual morphology in New Testament manuscripts

**Relevance**: Confirms Greek source language limitation. Translators into dual languages must add information not present in Greek morphology.

### 1.9 WALS Online. (2013). "Feature 34: Occurrence of Nominal Plurality." *World Atlas of Language Structures*.

- **Animacy effects**: "In principle, obligatory occurrence of plural marking means that whenever plural reference is intended, the plural must be used. However, languages with otherwise obligatory plural marking very often allow it to be absent when the noun cooccurs with numerals."

### 1.10 WALS Online. (2013). "Feature 33: Coding of Nominal Plurality." *World Atlas of Language Structures*.

### 1.11 Sursurunga Language. (2024). *Wikipedia*.

**Citation Code**: {sursurunga-wiki}
**URL**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sursurunga_language

**Key Findings**:
- **Five-way number**: Singular, Dual, Paucal (lesser), Paucal (greater), Plural
- **Not true trial**: "These numbers, which only occur on pronouns, indicate a minimum of three and four, not exactly three and four the way the dual indicates exactly two."
- **Reanalysis**: "Some languages that have previously been described as having a quadral, like Sursurunga, have since been reanalyzed as having a paucal instead."
- **Corbett's terminology**: "Lesser paucal" and "greater paucal" instead of trial/quadral

**Relevance**: Clarifies trial vs. paucal distinction. Many claimed "trial" languages actually have paucal.

### 1.12 Larike Trial Number. *Linguistic Typology*.

**Citation Code**: {larike-trial}
**URL**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_number (cited)

**Key Findings**:
- **True trial**: "It should be stated explicitly that Larike trials are true trial forms. In other words, they represent the quantity three, and are not used to refer to the more vague notion of several, as is a paucal or limited plural."

**Relevance**: Confirms existence of true trial. Establishes pronoun vs. noun distinction.

### 1.17 Malay Grammar. (2024). *Wikipedia*.

**Citation Code**: {malay-grammar}
**URL**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malay_grammar

**Key Findings**:
- **No grammatical plural**: "There is no grammatical plural in Malay. Thus *orang* may mean either 'person' or 'people'."

### 1.18 Animacy Hierarchy Effects. Woolford (2000). *UMass Linguistics*.

**Citation Code**: {woolford-2000-animacy}
**URL**: https://people.umass.edu/ellenw/Woolford%20Animacy%20Hierarchy%20Effects.pdf

**Relevance**: Explains why some languages mark number on "God" (human-like) but not "stones" (inanimate). Implications for TBTA frequency analysis.

## 3. Translation Case Studies

### 3.1 Fijian and Genesis 1:26 (Hypothetical)

**Language**: Fijian (Austronesian)
**Feature**: Suspected dual/trial in pronouns
**Verse**: Genesis 1:26 "Let us make man"

**Challenge**: Does Fijian have trial ("we-three") or only dual ("we-two") + plural ("we-many")?

**Correct Translation**:
- **If Trial exists**: Use trial → "Let us-three make" (explicit Trinity)
- **If only Dual + Plural**: Use plural → "Let us-many make" (orthodox but vague)
- **FORBIDDEN**: Never use dual → "Let us-two make" (Binitarian heresy)

**Insight**: Trial languages can be theologically more precise than Hebrew itself.

### 3.2 Hawaiian and Ruth 1 (Pair of Women)

**Language**: Hawaiian (Austronesian, Polynesian)
**Feature**: Suspected dual
**Verse**: Ruth 1 "Ruth and Naomi went together"

**Challenge**: Hebrew uses plural, but semantic count is exactly 2.

**Correct Translation**:
- **If Dual exists**: Use dual pronoun "they-two went" (narrative clarity)
- **If only Plural**: Use plural "they went" (less precise but acceptable)

**Insight**: Dual adds narrative precision, helps readers track participants.

### 3.3 Arabic and Paucal ("A Few People")

**Language**: Arabic (Standard)
**Feature**: Dual + Paucal (3-10) + Plural (11+)
**Verse**: Matthew 4:21 "And going on from there he saw two other brothers" vs. Acts 1:15 "The company of persons was in all about 120"

**Challenge**: Arabic can distinguish:
- **Dual**: اثنان (ithnān) for exactly 2
- **Paucal**: Used for 3-10
- **Plural**: Used for 11+

**Correct Translation**:
- Matthew 4:21 "two brothers" → **Dual** (exactly 2)
- Acts 1:15 "120 persons" → **Plural** (11+)

**Insight**: Paucal is rare but functionally useful for small groups.

### 3.4 Indonesian and Optional Number (Genesis 1:27)

**Language**: Indonesian
**Feature**: General number (optional marking)
**Verse**: Genesis 1:27 "So God created **man** in his own image...male and female he created **them**"

**Challenge**: "Man" is singular in Hebrew, but semantically refers to humanity (Adam = mankind).

**Indonesian Approach**:
- **Bare noun**: "manusia" (general number, neither singular nor plural)
- **Reduplication**: "manusia-manusia" (explicitly plural, optional)
- **Context**: Rely on "male and female" to clarify plurality

**Insight**: General number languages can handle semantic ambiguity without morphological marking.
