# Research: Voice Systems and Surface Realization

**Feature**: Surface Realization (Noun, Pronoun, Zero, Clitic)
**Research Date**: 2025-11-25
**Scope**: Voice systems, argument structure, symmetrical voice, antipassive/inverse, biblical translation
**Connection**: Voice systems determine which semantic arguments get promoted to syntactic subject position, which then affects how those arguments are realized on the surface (noun vs. pronoun vs. zero vs. clitic).

## Executive Summary

Voice systems determine how languages map semantic roles (agent, patient) onto syntactic positions (subject, object), which directly impacts surface realization patterns. This research examines voice typology from passive/active systems (English, Greek, Hebrew) through symmetrical voice (Philippine-type languages like Tagalog) to direct/inverse systems (Algonquian). Key findings: (1) Voice affects argument prominence and therefore surface realization choices; (2) Antipassive and inverse systems background different participants, changing which arguments appear as full nouns vs. pronouns vs. zero; (3) Biblical Hebrew Niphal functions primarily as middle voice, not passive; (4) Greek middle voice in NT requires careful translation (1 Cor 13:8); (5) Symmetrical voice languages challenge universal definitions of passive; (6) Pro-drop languages use zero subjects as routine reference while non-pro-drop languages require pronouns. Typological databases (WALS Features 107A, 108A, 108B; Grambank GB147, GB148) document cross-linguistic distribution. Translation challenges arise in passages like Acts 2:38 (passive baptism), Ephesians 2:8 (passive salvation), and Matthew 28:19 (participle "going").

## 1. Scholarly Sources on Voice Systems

### Shibatani, Masayoshi (ed.) (1988). _Passive and Voice_. Typological Studies in Language 16. John Benjamins

**Citation Code**: {shibatani-1988-passive-voice}

**Key Findings**:
- Voice phenomena have conceptual bases rooted in human cognition of actions
- Traditional definitions focus on active-passive but ignore active-middle opposition
- Active-middle opposition entails fundamental conceptual/meaning differences
- Transitivity is integral to the study of voice
- Impersonal passives often ignored in traditional definitions
- Voice systems vary significantly across language families

**Relevance**:
- Establishes theoretical framework for understanding voice beyond simple active/passive
- Middle voice distinction critical for Greek NT interpretation
- Conceptual grounding helps predict voice behavior across languages

**Source**: [Passive and Voice | Edited by Masayoshi Shibatani](https://benjamins.com/catalog/tsl.16)

### Givón, T. (ed.) (1994). _Voice and Inversion_. Typological Studies in Language 28. John Benjamins

**Citation Code**: {givon-1994-voice-inversion}

**Key Findings**:
- Four major voice constructions: direct-active, inverse, passive, antipassive
- Pragmatics of de-transitive voice surveyed using narrative texts
- Voice constructions have functional and typological aspects
- Grammar is not empty rules but instrument for constructing coherent communication
- Passive and antipassive affect transitivity differently

**Relevance**:
- Framework for understanding voice as pragmatic/discourse phenomenon
- Distinguishes four major voice types relevant to Bible translation
- Functional approach aligns with discourse-based translation theory

**Source**: [Voice and Inversion | Edited by T. Givón](https://benjamins.com/catalog/tsl.28)

### Klaiman, M.H. (1988). "Affectedness and control: A typology of voice systems." In _Passive and Voice_, 25-84

**Citation Code**: {klaiman-1988-affectedness}

**Key Findings**:
- Voice best understood through interaction of 'controller' vs 'affected' participant status
- Three-fold classification: passivization (derived voice), active-middle (basic voice), pragmatic voice (focus/inverse)
- Abstract hierarchies of potential control and actual control characterize participant roles
- Cross-linguistically applicable definition of voice based on participant conceptualization

**Relevance**:
- Provides theoretical basis for predicting voice behavior
- Affectedness hierarchy helps understand when agents are demoted
- Controller/affected distinction maps onto subject/object realization patterns

**Source**: [a typology of voice systems: Affectedness and control](https://benjamins.com/catalog/tsl.16.05kla)

### Klaiman, M.H. (1991). _Grammatical Voice_. Cambridge Studies in Linguistics 59. Cambridge University Press

**Citation Code**: {klaiman-1991-grammatical-voice}

**Key Findings**:
- First typological study of voice systems based on multi-language survey
- Voice phenomena characterized by controller/affected participant interaction
- Provides cross-linguistically applicable definitions
- Significant contribution to grammatical theory

**Relevance**:
- Comprehensive typological framework for voice analysis
- Helps classify voice systems in source and target Bible translation languages
- Theoretical grounding for algorithm development

**Source**: [Grammatical Voice (Cambridge Studies in Linguistics)](https://www.amazon.com/Grammatical-Voice-Cambridge-Studies-Linguistics/dp/0521369045)

### Hopper, Paul J. & Thompson, Sandra A. (1980). "Transitivity in Grammar and Discourse." _Language_ 56(2): 251-299

**Citation Code**: {hopper-thompson-1980-transitivity}

**Key Findings**:
- Transitivity involves multiple components beyond object presence
- Components include punctuality, telicity, agent activity, object affectedness, referentiality
- Transitivity is a continuum, not binary transitive/intransitive
- High transitivity correlates with foregrounding; low transitivity with backgrounding
- Grammatical/semantic prominence derives from discourse function

**Relevance**:
- Transitivity affects voice alternations (passive reduces transitivity)
- Foregrounding/backgrounding connects to participant tracking and surface realization
- Discourse function predicts when agents/patients appear as full nouns vs. reduced forms

**Source**: [Transitivity in Grammar and Discourse](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Transitivity-in-Grammar-and-Discourse-Hopper-Thompson/004f572145899873d87182a854eb5cd461834fa5)

### Dixon, R.M.W. (1994). _Ergativity_. Cambridge Studies in Linguistics 69. Cambridge University Press

**Citation Code**: {dixon-1994-ergativity}

**Key Findings**:
- Universal syntactic relations: S (intransitive subject), A (transitive agent), O (transitive object)
- Ergative languages mark A differently from S, grouping S with O
- ~25% of world's languages show ergative properties
- Morphological vs. syntactic ergativity must be distinguished
- Ergativity interacts with voice systems (especially antipassive)

**Relevance**:
- Ergative alignment affects which arguments get promoted/demoted
- S/A/O framework provides language-independent description
- Critical for understanding antipassive (ergative → absolutive shift)

**Source**: [Ergativity](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/ergativity/8BE12285BBED925F08EB74872C107017)

### Croft, William (2001). _Radical Construction Grammar: Syntactic Theory in Typological Perspective_. Oxford University Press

**Citation Code**: {croft-2001-rcg}

**Key Findings**:
- Chapter 8: "The Voice Continuum"
- No universal structural definition of passive and inverse constructions
- Syntactic properties defining passive/inverse don't coincide across languages
- Must construct syntactic space representing continuum of voice properties
- Basic elements are syntactic-semantic "Gestalts," not purely syntactic

**Relevance**:
- Challenges universal definitions; requires language-specific analysis
- Voice is a continuum, not discrete categories

**Source**: [The Voice Continuum | Radical Construction Grammar](https://academic.oup.com/book/32815/chapter-abstract/274874727)

**Citation Code**: {payne-1997-morphosyntax}

**Key Findings**:

## 3. Antipassive Voice

### Polinsky, Maria (2017). "Antipassive." In Coon, Massam & Travis (eds.), _The Oxford Handbook of Ergativity_. Oxford University Press

**Citation Code**: {polinsky-2017-antipassive}

**Key Findings**:
- Antipassive: logical object not realized as direct object
- No principled correlation between ergativity and antipassive
- Transitive/antipassive alternation more visible in ergative languages
- Typically involves change from ergative to absolutive case
- Antipassives more widespread than previously thought

**Relevance**:
- Antipassive backgrounds patient, affecting surface realization
- Ergative languages may have different subject realization patterns
- Helps understand voice systems beyond accusative alignment

**Source**: [1 1 Antipassive1 Maria Polinsky Abstract](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/mpolinsky/files/antipassive_new.pdf)

### Janic, Katarzyna & Witzlack-Makarevich, Alena (2021). "Antipassives in Crosslinguistic Perspective." _Annual Review of Linguistics_ 7: 109-130

**Citation Code**: {janic-witzlack-2021-antipassive}

**Key Findings**:
- Antipassives found in 70 languages from 25 families across 4 macro-areas
- Based on function, not just form
- Antipassives convey aspectual, modal, temporal information
- Preferred in imperfective, inceptive, potential, purposive, non-past contexts
- More widespread than traditional descriptions suggested

**Relevance**:
- Aspectual/modal connections help predict when antipassive appears
- Cross-linguistic distribution shows antipassive not limited to ergative languages
- Functional approach connects to TBTA's discourse-based analysis

**Source**: [Antipassives in Crosslinguistic Perspective | Annual Reviews](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/10.1146/annurev-linguistics-011619-030412)

## 4. Inverse Voice Systems

### DeLancey, Scott (1981). "An Interpretation of Split Ergativity and Related Patterns." _Language_ 57(3): 626-657

**Citation Code**: {delancey-1981-inverse}

**Key Findings**:
- Inverse marking: O argument outranks A on person hierarchy
- Speech act participants (SAPs) outrank 3rd persons
- Passive is device for indicating marked viewpoint (non-Agent perspective)
- Hierarchical agreement related to obviation
- Obviative NPs marked for low animacy/discourse prominence

**Relevance**:
- Person hierarchy affects which arguments are realized overtly
- Viewpoint marking connects to participant tracking
- Helps understand why certain arguments appear as zero vs. overt

**Source**: [078: Inverse / Topic](https://pages.uoregon.edu/delancey/sb/LECT7-8.htm)

### Jacques, Guillaume & Antonov, Anton (2014). "Direct/Inverse Systems." _Language and Linguistics Compass_ 8(7): 301-318

**Citation Code**: {jacques-antonov-2014-inverse}

**Key Findings**:
- Algonquian family represents prototype of inverse systems
- Direction marking relatively stable in Algonquian
- Person hierarchy: 2 > 1 > 3proximate > 3obviative (in Ojibwe)
- Direct/inverse distinction orthogonal to active/passive
- Inverse marking appears quite stable in at least some families

**Relevance**:
- Person-based systems affect argument realization
- Stability suggests cognitive/communicative motivation
- Relevant for languages with person-based voice systems

**Source**: [Direct/Inverse Systems - HAL](https://hal.science/hal-01386706/document)

### Oxford, Will (2023). "A tale of two inverses." _Syntax_ 26(3)

**Citation Code**: {oxford-2023-two-inverses}

**Key Findings**:
- Functional-typological literature distinguishes two inverse types
- Inverse voice: patient becomes subject
- Inverse alignment: patient agreed with like canonical subject
- Algonquian languages show both: "deep" voice (two 3rd persons), "shallow" alignment (3rd on SAP)

**Relevance**:
- Two distinct mechanisms for inverse both affect surface realization
- Voice vs. alignment distinction critical for analysis
- Helps understand complex interactions in person-based systems

**Source**: [A tale of two inverses](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/synt.12251)

## 7. Surface Realization and Pro-Drop

**Citation Code**: {pro-drop-parameter}

**Key Findings**:
- Pro-drop parameter determines if subject can be suppressed
- Italian/Spanish can have subject-less declarative sentences
- English/French must have overt subject (even dummy subjects)
- Information structure and discourse factors determine null realization
- Accessibility/salience of antecedent crucial for null arguments
- Italian children realize overtly discursively informative arguments, drop uninformative ones

**Relevance**:
- Pro-drop directly affects surface realization (zero vs. pronoun)
- Discourse accessibility predicts when arguments can be null
- Cross-linguistic variation requires language-specific analysis

**Source**: [Null-subject language - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null-subject_language)

## 9. Bible Translation Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Tagalog (Philippine-type, Symmetrical Voice)

**Language**: Tagalog (Austronesian, Philippines)
**ISO 639-3**: tgl
**Voice System**: Symmetrical voice with six voices (Actor, Patient, Locative, Benefactive, Instrument, Reason)

**Translation Context**:
- Philippine Bible Society has multiple Tagalog translations
- *Ang Biblia* (1905) - formal, equivalent to ASV
- *Magandang Balita Biblia* (1973/1980) - dynamic, equivalent to GNB

**Voice Challenges**:
- Essential difference from European languages: "triple passive form"
- Word order: predicate before subject
- Voice affixes (an, in, i) create multiple passive-like forms
- No single "active voice" equivalent to English
- Translation must respect target language voice preferences

**Key Passage**: Acts 2:38 (passive imperative "be baptized")
- Greek: βαπτισθήτω (aorist passive imperative)
- Tagalog must choose appropriate voice based on focus
- Patient voice if focus on person being baptized
- Actor voice if focus on baptizer

**Relevance**:
- Symmetrical voice systems don't map one-to-one with Greek active/passive
- Voice choice based on discourse focus, not just semantic roles
- Surface realization follows voice: focused argument = noun, others may be reduced

**Sources**:
- [Bible translations into the languages of the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible_translations_into_the_languages_of_the_Philippines)
- [The Pinoy Version: A Revelation](https://translation.bible/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/del-corro-2020-the-pinoy-version-a-revelation.pdf)

### Case Study 2: Biblical Hebrew (Pro-Drop, Middle Voice Prominent)

**Language**: Biblical Hebrew (Semitic)
**Voice System**: Qal (basic), Niphal (middle/passive), Piel/Pual (intensive), Hiphil/Hophal (causative)

**Surface Realization Pattern**:
- Pro-drop language: ~50% zero subjects in narrative
- Verb morphology encodes person/number/gender
- Full noun: ~30% (new/restaged participants)
- Pronoun: ~20% (explicit for emphasis/disambiguation)

**Voice Interaction**:
- Niphal primarily middle voice, secondarily passive
- Middle: subject concerned with self (undifferentiated)
- Passive: only when external agent present
- Surface realization: agent in passive can be preverbal (prominent) or oblique or absent

**Key Passage**: Genesis 1:27 - "So God created (Qal) man... male and female he created (Qal) them"
- Active voice, pro-drop language
- Subject "God" stated first mention, then null in second clause
- Object "man/them" varies: full noun → pronoun

**Translation Challenge**:
- English (non-pro-drop) must supply "he" where Hebrew has zero
- Voice affects which argument is subject, then pro-drop determines surface form

**Relevance**:
- Source language voice system interacts with pro-drop
- Niphal as middle (not passive) changes argument structure
- Surface realization follows participant tracking + pro-drop rules

**Source**: [The Niphal as middle voice](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0309089217743160)

### Case Study 3: Greek New Testament (Partial Pro-Drop, Three-Way Voice)

**Language**: Koine Greek (Indo-European)
**Voice System**: Active, Middle, Passive (plus medio-passive conflation)

**Surface Realization Pattern**:
- Partial pro-drop: ~35% zero subjects
- Noun: ~35%, Pronoun: ~30%, Zero: ~35%
- Voice affects realization: passive promotes patient to subject position

**Voice-Specific Patterns**:

**Active Voice**:
- Agent = subject (overt or null based on discourse)
- Patient = object (accusative case)
- Example: John 3:16 - "God loved (active) the world"
  - ἠγάπησεν (active aorist) - agent "God" overt, patient "world" accusative

**Middle Voice**:
- Subject as agent and experiencer
- 3,726 middle forms in NT
- 11 semantic types (reflexive, reciprocal, mental activity, etc.)
- Example: 1 Corinthians 13:8 - "tongues will cease (middle)"
  - παύσονται (future middle) - implies self-cessation, not external agent

**Passive Voice**:
- Patient promoted to subject
- Agent either absent or oblique (ὑπό + genitive)
- Example: Acts 2:38 - "be baptized (passive)"
  - βαπτισθήτω (aorist passive imperative) - patient as subject, agent implied
- Example: Ephesians 2:8 - "you have been saved (perfect passive)"
  - σεσῳσμένοι (perfect passive participle) - patient focus, God as implied agent

**Translation Implications**:
- Middle voice often lost in English (rendered as active or passive)
- Perfect passive emphasizes completed state continuing to present
- Pro-drop allows zero subjects where English requires pronouns

**Key Theological Passages**:

**1. Acts 2:38** - Passive voice baptism
- Greek passive imperative: patient submits to being baptized by another
- Agent not self (baptism not self-administered)
- Theological implication: baptism involves community (others present)

**2. Ephesians 2:8** - Passive voice salvation
- Perfect passive participle: completed action with ongoing results
- Passive emphasizes God as agent, believer as recipient
- "You have been saved" - not self-salvation

**3. Matthew 28:19** - Participle "going"
- πορευθέντες (aorist passive participle) - not imperative
- Better: "as you go" or "while going"
- Main imperative: μαθητεύσατε "make disciples"

**4. 1 Corinthians 13:8** - Middle voice "cease"
- παύσονται (middle) vs. καταργηθήσονται (passive) for prophecies/knowledge
- Middle implies tongues "stop of themselves" without external agent
- Theological debate: cessationist vs. continuationist interpretations

**Relevance**:
- Three-way voice system (active/middle/passive) affects argument structure
- Voice determines which semantic role = syntactic subject
- Surface realization then follows from pro-drop rules applied to grammatical subject
- Middle voice creates nuances lost in two-way English system

## 10. Key Biblical Passages Where Voice Affects Translation

### Genesis 1:26 - "Let us make man in our image"

**Voice Issue**: Active voice, first person plural
**Surface Realization**: Full noun "God" (v.1), then null subject (pro-drop Hebrew)
**Translation Challenge**:
- "Us/our" = Trial number (Trinity: Father, Son, Spirit)
- Voice: active (God as agent)
- Pro-drop: subject null in verb-initial clause
- Non-pro-drop languages must supply "we"

**Relevance**: Voice (active) + number (trial) + surface realization (null subject in Hebrew, overt in English)

### Genesis 22:2 - "Take your son... and offer him"

**Voice Issue**: Active → (implied) passive/middle
**Hebrew**: Qal imperative + Hiphil imperative (causative)
**Surface Realization**: Subject (Abraham) null in second verb (pro-drop)
**Translation Challenge**:
- Voice shift: take (active) → cause to ascend = offer (causative)
- Object "him" (Isaac) becomes patient of offering
- Some translations passive-ize: "let him be offered"

**Relevance**: Voice alternation affects surface realization of Isaac (object vs. subject)

### Acts 2:38 - "Repent and be baptized"

**Voice Issue**: Active imperative (μετανοήσατε) + Passive imperative (βαπτισθήτω)
**Greek**: Active = self-directed action; Passive = other-directed action
**Surface Realization**:
- Repentance: subject = agent (you repent yourself)
- Baptism: subject = patient (you are baptized by another)

**Translation Challenge**:
- Passive implies agent other than subject (someone baptizes you)
- Theological: baptism not self-administered
- Languages without passive must use circumlocution ("let someone baptize you")

**Relevance**: Voice determines agent-patient roles, affecting who appears in subject position

### Romans 8:28 - "All things work together for good"

**Voice Issue**: Active (συνεργεῖ) - who is subject?
**Greek**: Either "all things work together" or "God works all things together"
**Surface Realization**: Subject ambiguous - "all things" or "God" (null)
**Translation Variants**:
- "All things work together" (things = subject)
- "God works all things together" (God = null subject)
- "God causes all things to work together" (causative)

**Relevance**: Voice and null subjects interact - different subject = different surface realization

### Ephesians 2:8 - "By grace you have been saved"

**Voice Issue**: Perfect passive participle (σεσῳσμένοι)
**Greek**: Perfect = completed action with ongoing results; Passive = God as agent
**Surface Realization**:
- Patient (you) = grammatical subject
- Agent (God) implied, not stated
- Passive emphasizes recipient role

**Translation Challenge**:
- English passive maintains structure
- Active-only languages must add agent: "God has saved you"
- Perfect aspect: state persists from past action

**Relevance**: Passive promotes patient to subject; voice choice affects theological emphasis (recipient vs. agent focus)

### Matthew 28:19 - "Go therefore and make disciples"

**Voice Issue**: Passive participle (πορευθέντες) + Active imperative (μαθητεύσατε)
**Greek**:
- "Going" = aorist passive participle (temporal/manner)
- "Make disciples" = aorist active imperative (main command)

**Surface Realization**:
- Traditional: "Go" (imperative) - makes going the command
- Accurate: "As you go" / "While going" (participle) - going is circumstantial

**Translation Challenge**:
- Participle vs. imperative distinction
- Only one imperative in Greek: μαθητεύσατε "make disciples"
- Three participles modify it: going, baptizing, teaching

**Relevance**: Voice (passive participle) signals subordinate action, not main command

### 1 Corinthians 13:8 - "Love never fails... tongues will cease"

**Voice Issue**: Middle (παύσονται) vs. Passive (καταργηθήσονται)
**Greek**:
- Prophecies/knowledge: καταργηθήσονται (future passive) - "will be done away with" (external agent)
- Tongues: παύσονται (future middle) - "will cease" (self-cessation, no external agent)

**Surface Realization**:
- Passive: agent implied (God will end prophecy/knowledge)
- Middle: no external agent (tongues stop on their own)

**Translation Challenge**:
- English loses middle voice distinction
- Theological debate: does middle imply tongues ended naturally (cessationism)?
- Wallace: middle = "cease of their own accord" or "die out"

**Relevance**: Voice distinction (middle vs. passive) signals presence/absence of external agent

## 11. Agent Prominence and Patient Demotion

### Riesberg, Sonja et al. (2015). "Agent prominence in symmetrical voice languages." _STUF - Language Typology and Universals_ 68(2): 159-184

**Citation Code**: {riesberg-2015-agent-prominence}

**Key Findings**:
- Morpho-syntactically agents universally more prominent than patients (claimed)
- Some languages deviate: Tima (split ergative), Totoli (symmetrical voice)
- Symmetrical voice languages challenge agent prominence universals
- Different languages code prominence differently

**Relevance**:
- Agent prominence affects surface realization (agents more likely overt)
- Symmetrical voice systems redistribute prominence
- Helps predict which arguments appear as full NPs

**Source**: [Agent prominence in symmetrical voice languages](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/stuf-2015-0023/html?lang=en)

### Mykhaylyk, Roksolana & Sopata, Aldona (2020). "Agent demotion and patient promotion do not affect prominence in upcoming discourse: Results of a continuation task for Polish passives and impersonals." _Slavia_ 89(1): 75-95

**Citation Code**: {mykhaylyk-sopata-2020-polish}

**Key Findings**:
- Passives demote agents; personal passives also promote patients
- Agent demotion and patient promotion have only local effects (within clause)
- Do NOT affect prominence structure of upcoming discourse
- Challenges discourse persistence predictions

**Relevance**:
- Voice alternations affect local surface realization
- But don't necessarily change discourse tracking patterns
- Participant tracking may be independent of voice

**Source**: [Agent demotion and patient promotion do not affect prominence](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/slaw-2025-0020/html)

## Executive Summary: Top 10 Findings

### 1. Voice Systems Map Semantic Roles to Syntactic Positions

**Finding**: Voice determines which semantic role (agent, patient, experiencer) gets promoted to syntactic subject position. This mapping directly affects surface realization because grammatical subjects and objects have different realization patterns (noun vs. pronoun vs. zero).

**Implication**: To predict surface realization, must first understand voice system of source and target languages.

**Sources**: {klaiman-1988-affectedness}, {vanvalin-2005-interface}, {dixon-1994-ergativity}

---

### 2. Symmetrical Voice Languages Challenge Universal Definitions

**Finding**: Philippine-type languages have "symmetrical voice" where multiple transitive constructions are equally unmarked, with different arguments serving as syntactic pivot without demotion to oblique. This challenges traditional active/passive dichotomy and universal definitions of voice.

**Implication**: Translation into Tagalog, Malay, Malagasy, etc. cannot assume Western active/passive patterns. Voice choice based on discourse focus, not semantic roles alone.

**Sources**: {foley-2008-philippine-voice}, {himmelmann-2013-totoli}, {austin-musgrave-2008-austronesian}, {croft-2001-rcg}

---

### 3. Biblical Hebrew Niphal is Middle Voice, Not Primarily Passive

**Finding**: Traditional grammar treats Niphal as "passive of Qal," but modern scholarship shows it predominantly marks middle voice (subject concerned with self as undifferentiated middle). Passive reading only occurs when external agent is present and non-coreferential.

**Implication**: Hebrew "passive" doesn't map one-to-one with Greek/English passive. Middle voice interpretation changes argument structure and affects translation decisions.

**Sources**: {vanwolde-2019-niphal-middle}, {benton-2013-niphal-aspect}, {callaham-2010-passive-paradox}

---

### 4. Greek NT Middle Voice Lost in English Translation

**Finding**: Greek has three-way voice distinction (active, middle, passive) with 3,726 middle voice forms in NT. Middle voice indicates subject as both agent and experiencer. English typically reduces middle to either active or passive, losing semantic nuance.

**Implication**: Critical NT passages (1 Cor 13:8, many deponent verbs) have middle voice semantics not captured in English. Translation must compensate for lost distinction.

**Sources**: {allan-nt-middle}, {wallace-greek-middle}

**Key Passage**: 1 Corinthians 13:8 - "tongues will cease (middle)" vs. "prophecies will be done away with (passive)" - middle implies self-cessation without external agent, affecting cessationist theology.

---

### 5. Antipassive More Widespread Than Previously Recognized

**Finding**: Antipassives found in 70 languages from 25 families across 4 macro-areas, not limited to ergative languages as traditionally thought. Antipassives convey aspectual/modal/temporal information and are preferred in imperfective, potential, purposive, non-past contexts.

**Implication**: Languages with antipassive require understanding of when it's used. Antipassive backgrounds patient, affecting surface realization (patient becomes oblique or null rather than direct object).

**Sources**: {janic-witzlack-2021-antipassive}, {polinsky-2017-antipassive}

---

### 6. Inverse Voice Systems Follow Person Hierarchy

**Finding**: Inverse-marking languages rank persons (2 > 1 > 3proximate > 3obviative in Algonquian). Verb marked "inverse" when object outranks agent on hierarchy. Two types: inverse voice (patient becomes subject) and inverse alignment (patient agreed with like subject).

**Implication**: Person-based voice systems affect which arguments are overt vs. null. Speech act participants (1st/2nd person) pattern differently from 3rd person, affecting surface realization.

**Sources**: {delancey-1981-inverse}, {jacques-antonov-2014-inverse}, {oxford-2023-two-inverses}

---

### 7. Pro-Drop Interacts with Voice to Determine Surface Realization

**Finding**: Pro-drop languages (70% of world's languages) allow null subjects when recoverable from context. Voice determines which semantic role = syntactic subject, then pro-drop rules determine if that subject appears as noun, pronoun, or zero. Biblical Hebrew ~50% zero subjects, Greek ~35%, English ~5% (coordinated ellipsis only).

**Implication**: Cannot predict surface realization from voice alone. Must combine voice (which role is subject) with pro-drop status (how subjects are realized).

**Sources**: {pro-drop-parameter}, {frascarelli-2017-null-subjects}

**Example**: Genesis 1:27 - Hebrew active voice + pro-drop = null subject "God" in second clause. English active voice + non-pro-drop = overt "he."

---

### 8. Passive Voice Affects Theological Interpretation in NT

**Finding**: Greek passive voice promotes patient to subject position, backgrounding or eliminating agent. Perfect passive (σεσῳσμένοι "having been saved") emphasizes completed state persisting to present. Passive theologically emphasizes God's agency (implied agent) and believer's recipient role.

**Implication**: Voice choice affects theological emphasis. Acts 2:38 passive "be baptized" emphasizes community involvement (baptism by others). Ephesians 2:8 passive "you have been saved" emphasizes grace (God saves) not works (self-salvation).

**Sources**: Case Study 3 (Greek NT), {wals-voice}

**Key Passages**:
- Acts 2:38: βαπτισθήτω (passive imperative) - baptism not self-administered
- Ephesians 2:8: σεσῳσμένοι (perfect passive) - salvation by God, not self
- Romans 8:28: Textual variant affects whether God or "all things" is subject

---

### 9. Voice Continuum, Not Discrete Categories

**Finding**: Croft's Radical Construction Grammar shows voice properties form continuum across languages. No universal structural definition of passive/inverse. Must construct syntactic space representing continuum rather than assuming discrete categories apply cross-linguistically.

**Implication**: Algorithm cannot assume universal voice categories. Must learn language-specific patterns and recognize gradience in voice systems.

**Sources**: {croft-2001-rcg}, {hopper-thompson-1980-transitivity}

**Example**: Transitivity continuum (Hopper & Thompson) shows voice alternations position clauses along foregrounding/backgrounding scale, not binary transitive/intransitive.

---

### 10. Typological Databases Document Voice Distribution

**Finding**: WALS documents passive (Feature 107A) and antipassive (108A/108B) across languages. Grambank (2,467 languages) documents morphological passive (GB147) and antipassive (GB148), plus alignment types and TAM/verb class interactions. 25% of WALS sample has antipassive; 12 languages have both passive and antipassive.

**Implication**: Can predict which target languages require voice distinctions. WALS/Grambank IDs allow linking to language-specific voice data for algorithm training.

**Sources**: {wals-voice}, {grambank-voice}

**Relevant WALS Features**:
- 107A: Passive Constructions
- 108A: Antipassive Constructions
- 108B: Productivity of Antipassive

**Relevant Grambank Features**:
- GB147: Morphological passive on verb
- GB148: Morphological antipassive on verb
- GB409: Ergative alignment of flagging
- GB095: TAM-based variation in core marking
- GB096: Verb class-based variation in core marking
